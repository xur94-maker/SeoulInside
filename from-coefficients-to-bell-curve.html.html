From the Coefficient Table to the Bell Curve

The marbles move under a physics simulation of gravity, collisions with pins and air drag. Nothing decides in advance which way a marble bounces, yet the counts per bin pile up into a bell shape. The dots show the theory for a perfect fair coin (p = ½), so they differ slightly from the real result, because real marbles are not ideal coins. The fewer the pin rows, the bigger the gap. Tilting the board pushes the pile to one side, and the run stops automatically when a bin is full.

Each coin is a thin disc simulated as a 3D rigid body: gravity, spin, gyroscopic wobble, and impulse-based collisions with bounce and friction. Nothing decides heads or tails in advance; the face you see is simply the way the coin ends up lying. Every coin starts heads up. With randomness at 0%, all coins get an identical throw and land identically, because the physics is deterministic. As the random variation in launch speed, spin and tilt grows, the outcome becomes unpredictable, and the number of heads per trial piles up into the same bell shape as on the Galton board. The coins do not collide with each other. A real coin toss is known to land on its starting face very slightly more than half the time.
