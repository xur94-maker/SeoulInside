Before Maxwell's Equations — Faraday
Faraday could not solve differential equations.

He was born on September 22, 1791, in the slum of Newington Butts on the outskirts of London. His father was a blacksmith. He was sickly. He left school at 13. He received almost no formal education.

His education consisted of reading, writing, and the rudiments of arithmetic. He had almost no training in mathematics. "The son of a blacksmith, Faraday received a minimal education, which did not include much training in mathematics."

In 1804, he became an apprentice at a bookshop and bindery for eight years. While binding books, he read the article on "Electricity" in the third edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. It was 120 pages long. After reading it, he built a hand-cranked electrostatic generator. He also read Mrs. Marcet's *Conversations on Chemistry*. In 1858, in a letter to de la Rive, he wrote:

"These two books were the beginning of my philosophy. Mrs. Marcet's book gave me the foundation of my chemistry."

In 1812, a customer gave him four tickets to Humphry Davy's lectures at the Royal Institution. Faraday attended the lectures, organized his notes, and had them bound. He sent them to Davy along with a request for a job. Davy replied, but there was no position at the time. Then, not long after, Davy's assistant was fired after getting into a fight. Faraday took over the position.

On March 1, 1813, he began work at the Royal Institution. His title was effectively "bottle-washer." In his letter of recommendation, Davy wrote that Faraday was 22 years old and had "good habits, a lively and cheerful character, and intelligent conduct."

Davy has a famous line:

"My greatest discovery was Michael Faraday."

Yet according to other sources, Davy later accused Faraday of plagiarism. Because of this, Faraday stopped his electromagnetic research until Davy died.

From 1813 to 1815, Davy took his newlywed wife and Faraday on an 18-month scientific tour of Europe. Faraday's official status was "scientific assistant." But when Davy's valet refused to accompany them, Faraday had to double as a servant as well. Davy's wife came from aristocratic stock. She required Faraday to eat with the servants and sit outside the carriage. Faraday nearly quit, but endured it. A later biographer called this trip his "university." Following Davy, he met Europe's leading scientists — Ampère, Arago, Gay-Lussac, Volta, and others.

Faraday was humble. He declined a knighthood and declined the presidency of the Royal Society. "Faraday was an extremely modest person. For example, he declined honors bestowed in recognition of his accomplishments, such as a knighthood and the presidency of the Royal Society."

In 1831, Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction. In 1846, he speculated that light might be a vibration of lines of force. But he could not write that down as an equation. He scattered iron filings to make magnetic lines of force visible to the eye. Yet he had no way to express in equations what that meant.

In 1841, his health began to fail. In February 1843, he wrote to Matteucci:

"I received your letter yesterday. I was touched by your kind concern for a man who feels — as far as the world is concerned, as the world sees it — that his purpose is finished. … My health and spirits are well enough, but my memory has gone. This makes one withdraw into oneself, as if one were deaf."

In the latter half of 1844, a young woman of the London upper class asked to become his student and repeat all his experiments. He replied:

"What you have is unshaken physical and mental health, and all the confidence of youth. I am an old laborer who has stood long at the workbench, and I feel myself wearing out every day. … Many beautiful discoveries occur to my mind — things I once wanted to do and still want to do. But when I turn my thoughts to work done by hand, I lose all hope, seeing how slowly the work proceeds, and how short I am of time and of mental and physical strength."

In 1857, Faraday received Maxwell's paper. He wrote to Maxwell:

"I was almost frightened when I saw such mathematical force made to bear upon the subject, and then wondered to see that the subject stood it so well."

"I was frightened." This is the genuine reaction of a man who saw physics with his eyes, at the moment his own intuition was first "tested" by mathematics. He did not know whether mathematics would tear his intuition apart. It did not. His intuition "stood up to" the mathematics. But that mathematics was something he himself could not read.

In 1860, Maxwell came to visit him. Maxwell was 29. Faraday was approaching seventy. Maxwell handed him the paper he had written four years earlier, "On Faraday's Lines of Force." Faraday said two things to him.

The first was humility: "I do not think my doctrine is true, but you are the one who has truly understood it."

The second was a command:

"It's a fine paper. But you must not stop at explaining my ideas in mathematics. You must break through it!"

This line is worth reading again and again. What Faraday was saying was: don't just translate my "lines of force" into equations. Go, with equations, to places I myself could never reach.

On March 12, 1862, Faraday performed his last experiment. The last entry number in his laboratory notebook was 16041. Its purpose was to find whether a magnetic field affects the emission spectral lines of a light source. It failed. And he never experimented again. From 1820 to 1862, he never once broke off his laboratory notebook.

His laboratory notebook was "already a draft of a paper. It was polished enough that someone else could have organized it and published it."

What Faraday "could not" do
Faraday's entire formal education amounted to an elementary-school level. He himself said: "The education I received was only the most basic — reading, writing, and the rudiments of arithmetic."

His father was a blacksmith, and he left school at 13.

What he could not do was the "language" of mathematics. It wasn't that he couldn't do arithmetic. He could not "write" physics in equations. Around 1840, when he was studying electromagnetic force, he chose intuitive understanding over mathematical explanation. He introduced "lines of force" to "see" the distribution of the field, and used iron filings to "draw" the lines of magnetic force. Scientists trained in mathematics at the time found it genuinely difficult to understand his concept of lines of force, "because there was no mathematical proof."

He could "see" precisely because he "could not"
This is the most subtle point in this story.

Faraday's contribution to physics was not something "derived through mathematics." He saw with his eyes. There were two books he read while binding books at the bindery. One was the article on "Electricity" in the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, from which he got his first concepts of electricity. The other was Mrs. Marcet's *Conversations on Chemistry*, which laid the foundation for his lifelong science.

He had no equations to rely on. So he relied on other things — iron filings, compass needles, polarized light, the color of flames. With these things "visible to the naked eye," he saw the structure of lines of force that others could not see through mathematics.

Zeeman, who later won the Nobel Prize, said that the titles of Faraday's two foundational papers — "On the Magnetization of Light" and "Thoughts on Ray Vibrations" — "seem to our eyes almost prophetic." Faraday was, in fact, actually "seeing" light, not "calculating" it. On March 12, 1862, his last experiment sought to see whether a magnetic field changed the refractive index of spectral lines. The last line of his laboratory notebook reads: "In polarized or unpolarized rays, no effect whatever was observed."

He could not see it. But his intuition saw it. That effect was discovered by Zeeman 35 years later.

Maxwell was not his "translator" but his "breakthrough"
In 1860, Maxwell came to visit him, bringing the paper he had written four years earlier, "On Faraday's Lines of Force." Faraday said two things to him.

The first was humility: "I do not think my doctrine is true, but you are the one who has truly understood it."

The second was a command:

"It's a fine paper. But you must not stop at explaining my ideas in mathematics. You must break through it!"

This line is worth reading again and again. What Faraday was saying was: don't just translate my "lines of force" into equations. Go, with equations, to places I myself could never reach.

Maxwell understood. He did not remain at "explaining Faraday." He introduced displacement current, derived that the speed of electromagnetic waves equals the speed of light, and wrote twenty equations.

Maxwell himself said: "As the human mind has different types, so scientific truth must have different forms of expression." He was defending Faraday's "rough form." But what Faraday wanted was not defense — it was transcendence.

Maxwell's Completion
In 1865, Maxwell completed the equations of the electromagnetic field. There were twenty equations. Having introduced displacement current, he calculated the speed of electromagnetic waves and found it equal to the speed of light. Thus he discovered that light is electromagnetic radiation.

In 1873, Maxwell published *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism*. In the preface he wrote:

"Before I began the study of electricity I resolved to read no mathematics on the subject till I had first read through Faraday's *Experimental Researches in Electricity*. … I was aware that there was supposed to be a difference between Faraday's way of conceiving phenomena and that of the mathematicians, … but I was not convinced that this difference arose from one of them being wrong."

This book was dedicated to Faraday.

Heaviside — From Twenty to Four
In 1884, Oliver Heaviside reduced the twenty equations to four. These are the four we today call "Maxwell's equations."

Heaviside never attended university. He left school at 16. He worked as a telegraph operator, for the Danish Great Northern Telegraph Company. He was hearing-impaired, having had scarlet fever as a child. He became a recluse. From 1875 to 1900, he studied Maxwell while unemployed and living in his parents' home. He was treated as an "eccentric." His papers were long like soliloquies, and most scientists found them difficult to understand.

Yet what he did was:

Reduce twenty equations to four

Create vector calculus — div, grad, curl

Invent operational calculus — the prototype of the Laplace transform

Derive the telegraph equations — a practical problem of telegraph cables

Coin terminology — inductance, impedance, admittance, reactance, skin effect

Design the coaxial cable — patented in 1880

In 1893, he answered this question: "If there is reason to believe that he [Maxwell] would have admitted the necessity for the change when it was pointed out, then I think the resulting modified theory may fairly be called Maxwell's theory."

He had fixed everything himself, yet left the original author's name on it.

In 1922, he received the first Faraday Medal — a medal named after Faraday, and Heaviside was its very first recipient.

Even the name "Maxwell's Equations"
"The equations of electromagnetism have previously been known as the Hertz–Heaviside and Maxwell–Hertz equations, but the term 'Maxwell's equations' was popularized by Einstein in 1940, in his monograph *Considerations Concerning the Fundamentals of Theoretical Physics*."

In the late 19th century they were called the "Hertz–Heaviside equations" or the "Maxwell–Hertz equations." The term "Maxwell's equations" was spread by Einstein in 1940 in his own book.

In other words, even the name did not originally belong to Maxwell.

Einstein — "I stand on Maxwell's shoulders"
Einstein said, "I do not stand on the shoulders of Newton, but of Maxwell."

In 1922, at Cambridge, someone told him, "You stood on Newton's shoulders and did great things." Einstein replied:

"That is not quite right. I stood on Maxwell's shoulders."

This is not a legend. In photographs of Einstein's study in Princeton, a portrait of Maxwell hangs over his right shoulder. Only three British physicists' portraits hung in that study: Newton, Faraday, and Maxwell. No one else.

In 1931, for the centenary of Maxwell's birth, he wrote in a commemorative volume from Cambridge University Press:

"Before Maxwell, physical reality was thought of as consisting of material particles. … After Maxwell, physical reality was thought of as represented by continuous fields. … This change in the concept of reality is the most profound and fruitful one that physics has experienced since the time of Newton."

In 1925, a young Ukrainian Jewish woman named Esther Salaman attended Einstein's lectures on relativity in Berlin. Einstein told her:

"I do not think only of Newton. Without Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism there would be no modern physics. I owe more to Maxwell than to anyone."

He advised her to go to Cambridge and study physics. "England has always produced the best physicists."

In 1949 he wrote:

"The special theory of relativity owes its origins to Maxwell's equations of the electromagnetic field."

Why he said that
The title of Einstein's 1905 paper is "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies." Its first sentence reads:

"It is known that Maxwell's electrodynamics — as usually understood at the present time — when applied to moving bodies, leads to asymmetries which do not appear to be inherent in the phenomena."

What was that "asymmetry"? Einstein used the relative motion of a magnet and a conductor as his example.

If the magnet moves and the conductor is at rest: the conductor feels a changing magnetic field, and an induced current arises.

If the conductor moves and the magnet is at rest: charges within the conductor feel the Lorentz force, and an induced current arises.

The physical phenomenon is the same, because the relative motion of the conductor and magnet is the same. Yet Maxwell's theory explains it with two different equations. Einstein held that this "asymmetry" should not exist. A phenomenon should depend only on relative motion, not on "who is moving."

He did two things. He elevated the principle of relativity to an axiom. And he required that Maxwell's equations retain their form in every inertial frame. The only transformation that makes this possible is the Lorentz transformation. And the Lorentz transformation implies the invariance of the speed of light, the non-absoluteness of time, and the non-absoluteness of space.

In other words, special relativity was not something Einstein "invented." It was a conclusion he was forced to accept "in order to save Maxwell's equations."

One caveat is worth noting. The Michelson–Morley experiment was not needed here. Einstein himself said many times that this experiment had "no direct influence" on the development of relativity. What genuinely influenced him was the asymmetry within Maxwell's equations themselves, along with Fizeau's flowing-water experiment and stellar aberration.

"Einstein gave varying accounts of its influence... When volunteering explanations, Einstein consistently emphasized the role of the Fizeau water tube experiment and stellar aberration rather than Michelson's work."

Field versus Particle
The core of what Einstein prized in Maxwell is the concept of the "field."

Newton's universe is made of particles. There are forces between particles, and force acts instantaneously. Space and time are absolute.

Maxwell's universe is made of fields. Fields are continuous and propagate at a finite speed. Space and time may not be absolute.

Einstein's general relativity is, in essence, an extension of the "field" concept to gravity. Gravity is not a force acting instantaneously, but the curvature of spacetime.

Gravitational waves. When Einstein studied gravitational waves in 1916–1918, he was clearly modeling them on Maxwell's electromagnetic waves. What he was seeking was "the same kind of propagation effect that Maxwell had established for electromagnetic waves fifty years earlier."

Influence — First Layer: Special Relativity
Maxwell's equations have a certain property. They hold in a stationary reference frame, but when moved to a moving reference frame via a Galilean transformation, the form of the equations changes. This means that "who is moving" changes the form of the physical laws. Einstein regarded this as impossible.

His solution was to elevate the principle of relativity to an axiom, and to require that Maxwell's equations retain their form in every inertial frame. The only transformation that makes this possible is the Lorentz transformation, which implies the invariance of the speed of light, and the non-absoluteness of time and space.

Einstein wrote in 1949:

"The special theory of relativity owes its origins to Maxwell's equations of the electromagnetic field."

Influence — Second Layer: Gauge Field Theory
Maxwell's equations have a property: whatever transformation (gauge transformation) is applied to the vector potential A and the scalar potential φ, the electric field E and magnetic field B do not change. At first, this property seemed like mere mathematical redundancy. But it later turned out to be a structural requirement of all fundamental interactions.

Electromagnetism is a U(1) gauge theory. The weak interaction is SU(2). The strong interaction is SU(3). The Lagrangian of the Standard Model is, in essence, an extension of Maxwell's gauge principle to larger symmetry groups.

In quantum electrodynamics (QED), the photon is a U(1) gauge boson and is massless, because gauge invariance forbids a mass term. In the electroweak unification, the W and Z bosons acquire mass via the Higgs mechanism, yet gauge invariance is not broken. Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) has eight types of gluons, which are SU(3) gauge bosons.

In one sentence: Maxwell's equations are the first gauge field theory. Every fundamental interaction theory of the 20th century is an extension of it.

Influence — Third Layer: Technology
Maxwell derived from his equations that the speed of electromagnetic waves equals the speed of light, and predicted that light is an electromagnetic wave. After Hertz proved this experimentally in 1888, the entire electromagnetic spectrum was discovered by 1910 — radio waves, microwaves, X-rays, gamma rays.

Hertz's experimental apparatus was as follows: "He used an oscillator made of polished brass knobs, each connected to an induction coil and separated by a tiny gap over which sparks could leap." And "Hertz made a simple receiver of looped wire. At the ends of the loop were small knobs separated by a tiny gap."

He measured standing waves: "In one of his experiments Hertz aimed the irradiation at a wide metal sheet. The direct and reflected waves combined to form a standing wave. Moving the vibrator, Hertz found nodes and loops of the standing wave, he measured the distance between the nodes and determined the wave length."

And he calculated the speed of light: "calculating natural frequency of the vibrator, he determined the spreading speed of the electromagnetic wave: Magnitude equal to the velocity of light was yielded. This proved the electromagnetic nature of light."

Today's mobile phones, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, GPS, MRI, radar, even microwave ovens — all of it began from these four sentences.

One IEEE review put it this way: "Maxwell's equations are the foundation of nearly all modern electrical, electronic, and optical technology."

The Korean Physical Society's webzine wrote that "the discovery of radio waves itself was predicted and directed purely through theoretical research." At the center of that theory stand Maxwell's equations.

Influence — Fourth Layer: The "Unreasonable Effectiveness" of Mathematics and Physics
A paper from the Indian National Science Academy discusses the relationship between mathematics and physics. In it, Wigner's famous line is quoted: "The enormous usefulness of mathematics in the natural sciences is something bordering on the mysterious, and there is no rational explanation for it."

Maxwell's equations are a perfect example of this statement. When written in 1865, they were a purely theoretical construction. No experiment required that "displacement current" must exist. But self-consistency demanded it. And that predicted electromagnetic waves, proven 23 years later. And that required the Lorentz transformation, and that required gauge invariance, and that became the template for the Standard Model.

A Counterintuitive Fact
One IEEE article points out an important but often overlooked fact.

Maxwell himself did not believe that light could be generated electromagnetically. In fact, he was silent about electromagnetic waves and their generation and detection. Hertz's experiment proved Maxwell's theory almost ten years after Maxwell's death.

Another article points out: "The theory Maxwell proposed in the 1860s is completely different from what we use today — even though the final equations remain the same." Maxwell introduced displacement current but abandoned it ten years later.

This means the following: what we today call "Maxwell's equations" did not exist for Maxwell himself. They were reconstructed between the 1870s and 1890s by Hertz, FitzGerald, Lodge, and Heaviside — young physicists known as the "Maxwellians."

And even the name "Maxwell's equations" was spread by Einstein in 1940. In the late 19th century they were called the "Hertz–Heaviside equations."

Recent Trends — AI Solves the Equations Instead
Traditionally, Maxwell's equations required lengthy computation on supercomputers. Recently, research using neural networks to perform this computation instead has exploded.

A PINN (Physics-Informed Neural Network) is a method that embeds Maxwell's equations themselves into a neural network's loss function, so that the network learns to obey the physical laws even without data. From 2020 to 2025, 139 related papers were published.

An FNO (Fourier Neural Operator) is a neural network that, given a material distribution as input, instantly predicts the distribution of the electric and magnetic fields. It dramatically speeds up design processes that require repeated simulation.

The core point is not "solving the equations faster." It is "changing the very way the equations are posed." Where previously one problem was solved at a time, now a neural network learns an entire family of problems at once.

Recent Trends — Where the Classical Equations Break Down
Maxwell's equations work perfectly in the macroscopic world. But their limits show up in two domains.

The nanoscale. As electronic devices shrink to the nanometer level, quantum effects begin to appear. Classical Maxwell's equations cannot predict these effects. A 2026 book, *New Advances in Maxwell's Equations and Applications*, addresses this problem head-on, explaining that "as electronic devices shrink to the nanometer and sub-nanometer scale, classical Maxwell's equations reveal quantum effects." The direction of research is to correct the classical equations with quantum effects.

The complex-frequency domain. A Stanford research team in 2026 extended Maxwell's equations to complex frequencies, realizing "ideal optical anti-matter" effects considered impossible under conventional theory. They showed that a material regarded purely as lossy can, under certain conditions, exhibit an entirely different response.

Recent Trends — Extending the Equations Themselves
The most interesting trend is the attempt to add something that is not in Maxwell's equations.

Vacuum nonlinearity. Maxwell's equations assume the vacuum is a linear medium. But quantum electrodynamics (QED) predicts that the vacuum becomes nonlinear under strong electromagnetic fields. A 2026 paper in *Scientific Reports* proposes a nonlinear extension of Maxwell's equations, showing that this extension admits soliton solutions whose energy connects to Planck's constant.

Unification with gravity. A 2026 paper extended Maxwell's equations through a spacetime-geometric formalism, showing that electromagnetism and gravity naturally separate out from a single mathematical structure.

Axion electrodynamics. When a hypothetical particle called the axion couples to the electromagnetic field, new effects such as polarization rotation or birefringence appear. The possibility of observing this experimentally in topological insulators is under discussion.

Recent Trends — Viewing the Vacuum as a Fluid
Some researchers attempt to reinterpret Maxwell's equations themselves as an approximation of superfluid dynamics.

The electric field is the gradient of vacuum pressure. The magnetic field is vacuum vorticity. Charge is a local defect in vacuum density.

From this viewpoint, Lorentz invariance is not a fundamental symmetry but an emergent symmetry of the quasiparticle spectrum. This is not yet mainstream, but it raises a fundamental question: "why do Maxwell's equations work the way they do?"

Handing Off to FDTD
These four equations — no matter who wrote them — have one problem. They can almost never be solved analytically.

Plane waves, spherical waves, waveguides. Except for a few special cases found in textbooks, no analytical solution exists. So people began solving them directly on a grid.

In 1966, Yee proposed such a grid. That is FDTD.

This simulator runs Maxwell's equations directly on that grid. It fires a plane wave at a scatterer, observes the scattering pattern, and compares it against Mie theory.

Charge creates an electric field.

These four sentences were completed in 1865, as twenty equations. They were reduced to four in 1884. The name "Maxwell's equations" came about in 1940.

Faraday could not solve differential equations. So he saw with his eyes. Maxwell was good at differential equations. So he wrote twenty of them. Heaviside was very good at differential equations. So he reduced them to four. Einstein was good at differential equations. So he created the theory of relativity.

Three of these four people were good at calculus. Yet what those three caught up to was something seen by the one person who could not do calculus.

## 1. Maxwell's Equations (Differential Form)
### 1.1 Gauss's Law (Electric Field) 
**Physical meaning**: Charge creates an electric field.
**Unicode**

∇·E = ρ/ε₀



**LaTeX**
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}

**Mathematica**

Div[E[x, y, z], {x, y, z}] == rho/eps0

**Python (SymPy)**


import sympy as sp
x, y, z, t, eps0 = sp.symbols('x y z t epsilon_0')
rho = sp.Function('rho')(x, y, z, t)
Ex, Ey, Ez = [sp.Function(f'E_{c}')(x, y, z, t) for c in 'xyz']
gauss_E = sp.Eq(sp.diff(Ex, x) + sp.diff(Ey, y) + sp.diff(Ez, z), rho/eps0)
gauss_E

### 1.2 Gauss's Law (Magnetic Field) 

**Physical meaning**: Magnetic monopoles do not exist.

**Unicode**



∇·B = 0

**LaTeX**

\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0

**Mathematica**

Div[B[x, y, z], {x, y, z}] == 0

**Python (SymPy)**


Bx, By, Bz = [sp.Function(f'B_{c}')(x, y, z, t) for c in 'xyz']
gauss_B = sp.Eq(sp.diff(Bx, x) + sp.diff(By, y) + sp.diff(Bz, z), 0)

**Physical meaning**: A time-varying magnetic field creates an electric field.

**Unicode**



∇×E = -∂B/∂t

**LaTeX**

\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}

**Mathematica**

Curl[E[x, y, z], {x, y, z}] == -D[B[x, y, z, t], t]

**Python (SymPy)**


curlE_x = sp.diff(Ez, y) - sp.diff(Ey, z)
curlE_y = sp.diff(Ex, z) - sp.diff(Ez, x)
curlE_z = sp.diff(Ey, x) - sp.diff(Ex, y)
faraday = [
    sp.Eq(curlE_x, -sp.diff(Bx, t)),
    sp.Eq(curlE_y, -sp.diff(By, t)),
    sp.Eq(curlE_z, -sp.diff(Bz, t)),
]

## 8. Two-Potential Formalism (Kudryavtsev & Trashkeev) 

**Source**: Kudryavtsev & Trashkeev, arXiv:1206.0354 (2012)

## Chapter 1. Maxwell's Equations and the Yee Grid

### 1.1 Physical Meaning of the 2D TMz Approximation 

3D FDTD uses six field components; 2D TMz uses **only three components**, Ez, Hx, and Hy. Suitable for structures uniform along z (cylinders, waveguides).

**Physical validity**: Cylinders, waveguides, slits. Not suitable for spheres or arbitrary shapes.

### 1.2 TMz Equations

**Unicode**



∂Ez/∂t = (1/ε)(∂Hy/∂x − ∂Hx/∂y)
∂Hx/∂t = −(1/μ)∂Ez/∂y
∂Hy/∂t = (1/μ)∂Ez/∂x

**LaTeX**

\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial H_x}{\partial y}\right)
\frac{\partial H_x}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y}, \quad
\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{\mu}\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}

**Mathematica**

eq_hx = D[Hx[x, y, t], t] == -(1/mu)*D[Ez[x, y, t], y];
eq_hy = D[Hy[x, y, t], t] == (1/mu)*D[Ez[x, y, t], x];

**Python (SymPy)**

Ez = sp.Function('Ez')(x, y, t)
Hx = sp.Function('Hx')(x, y, t)

tmz_eq1 = sp.Eq(sp.diff(Ez, t), (1/eps)*(sp.diff(Hy, x) - sp.diff(Hx, y)))
tmz_eq2 = sp.Eq(sp.diff(Hx, t), -(1/mu)*sp.diff(Ez, y))
tmz_eq3 = sp.Eq(sp.diff(Hy, t), (1/mu)*sp.diff(Ez, x))
tmz_eq1, tmz_eq2, tmz_eq3

### 1.7 Divergence Preservation

**Unicode**

**LaTeX**

**Python (SymPy)**

def curl(F, vars):
    x, y, z = vars
    Fx, Fy, Fz = F
    return sp.Matrix([
        sp.diff(Fz, y) - sp.diff(Fy, z),
        sp.diff(Fx, z) - sp.diff(Fz, x),
        sp.diff(Fy, x) - sp.diff(Fx, y)
    ])
def div(F, vars):
    return sum(sp.diff(F[i], vars[i]) for i in range(3))
curl_F = curl(F, (x, y, z))
div_curl_F = div(curl_F, (x, y, z))
sp.simplify(div_curl_F)  # → 0
