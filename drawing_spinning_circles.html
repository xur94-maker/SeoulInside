Drawing Pictures with Spinning Circles

Each circle turns at a constant radius and a constant speed. Yet when you chain the circles tip to tail, the end point traces a path that is not a circle. Try changing the number of circles and the number of samples.

Raise gravity and the circles become arm segments with mass (proportional to radius), while joint motors try to follow the target angles. The motors do not compensate for gravity, so the arm sags and wobbles. The dotted line is the ideal path with no gravity; the solid line is the path the real arm draws. With gravity at 0 it is exactly the same as the sigma picture. Lowering the motor strength makes it sag more. Set the motor strength to 0 and the motors switch off completely: the arm becomes a free multi-link pendulum with only its initial angles and velocities (with gravity at 0 it moves by inertia alone).

View

The sum that chains arrows (Σ)

z(t) = Σ cₙ e^(i·n·t) Each term is one circle. The radius is |cₙ| and the speed is n. The teal circles in the picture above are these terms. The number of circles M sets how many terms are added, and “Choose circles” sets which terms are picked first.

The sum that finds the circles (Σ)

cₙ = (1/N) Σ z(tₖ) e^(−2πi·nk/N) Pick N points along the shape’s perimeter, spin them backward, add them up, and average. This is what the computer’s np.fft.fft(z)/N does. Lower N to 4 and there are too few points, so the shape is read as a different curve (aliasing).

Where raising N ends up (∫)

cₙ = (1/2π) ∫ z(t) e^(−i·n·t) dt Take the points infinitely dense and the sigma above becomes an integral. The further right you push the N slider, the closer the coefficients get to this exact value.

When gravity switches on: it is an arm, not circles

In the world of sigma, terms are simply added with no relation to one another, so even the largest circle always turns at a constant speed. In a real motor arm, however, joint k supports the weight of every segment attached after it. That is why the base joint takes the most torque. Here each joint’s motor is also set in proportion to its maximum gravity torque, so the angular sag is similar at every joint; the sag accumulates toward the tip, and the end point wobbles the most.

The equation of motion for this picture is M(θ)θ̈ + C(θ,θ̇) + g(θ) = τ. M is the inertia matrix through which the segments pull on one another, C holds the centrifugal and Coriolis terms, g is gravity, and τ is the motor torque. The motor is PD control tracking the target angle, plus centrifugal compensation only. Gravity compensation is deliberately left out, so the error is exactly 0 when g = 0 and sag appears when g > 0.

The largest circle slows on the way up and speeds up on the way down (more clearly with a weaker motor and stronger gravity). The fourth card in the statistics at the top shows this change in speed. A weaker motor means more sag, so the shape is badly distorted. The calculation includes every visible circle (radius 0.003 or more) as a segment with real mass and solves it with a 5th-order adaptive integrator (Dormand–Prince, relative error 10⁻¹²). If the computer cannot keep up, accuracy is not cut; the display is slowed instead, and that ratio appears on the statistics card. Motor strength is applied to all joints at the same ratio as the stiffness with which a joint bears its load, and 0 means the motors are off. An arm with the motors off conserves energy and moves chaotically. In a chaotic system even a tiny numerical error pulls trajectories apart a few seconds later, so the calculation runs on a fixed grid (1/240 second) independent of screen frames, so that every device produces the same trajectory.

Circle chain vs. a real pendulum

The arm has the same shape, but the rule of motion differs. On the left is the circle chain (the world of sigma), where every segment turns at a set speed. On the right is a multi-link pendulum moved by gravity (same number of segments and lengths as the left, and the same model as the arm above: uniform rods with mass proportional to length). The more segments, the sooner chaos shows. The orange line on the right is a pendulum started with its initial angles differing by only 0.001 radian.

Presets · Play

It is most fun to set the lengths yourself. Equal lengths are symmetric and give tidy patterns, while unequal lengths make the chain’s pattern more complex. The pendulum moves regularly for small start angles and unpredictably for large ones. Because all the speeds are integers, the chain always returns to a closed pattern, but the pendulum does not. (From segment 3 on, the speeds form an arithmetic sequence: 1, w, 2w−1, 3w−2…)
