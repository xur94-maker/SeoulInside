Symmetry and Ratios in Cubic Functions

Why does this happen?

Over dinner with a close friend, the conversation drifted, oddly enough, into the properties of cubic functions. My friend's question was whether cubic functions have some kind of consistent symmetry, the way quadratic functions do.

Quadratic functions show symmetry intuitively. Cubic functions may not show it as intuitively, but because the quadratic function you get by differentiating them clearly has symmetry, the cubic function — being in an integral relationship with it — also turns out to be symmetric. Interestingly, this topic apparently shows up often in Korea's college entrance exams.

So today, just for fun, I decided to write it up.

TL;DR A cubic function is point-symmetric about the point whose x-value is the x-coordinate of the vertex of its derivative (a quadratic function) — that is, about the point (x, f(x)) at that x-value. That point is also exactly where the line connecting the two extrema of the cubic meets the cubic itself.

1. Why Only Cubics? — A Comparison Across Degrees

Before understanding the symmetry of cubic functions, you should first ask this question.

"Why is it specifically cubic functions that are point-symmetric? Why are linear, quadratic, and quartic functions different?"

Answering this question first gives every proof that follows the context of "so this is why cubics are special."

DegreeSymmetryReason
LinearPoint symmetry (about every point)A straight line
QuadraticLine symmetry (about an axis)Only x² term survives
CubicPoint symmetry (about inflection point)x³ is odd, x² term can be eliminated
QuarticNo symmetry in generalx³ term remains, breaking symmetry

The key question is: "What's left after you remove the lower-degree term?"

For a cubic function, if you eliminate the x² term by translation, the x term stays behind. What's left is "x³ + x — only odd-degree terms," and that's an odd function. Hence point symmetry holds.

For a quartic function, even after eliminating the x³ term by translation, the x term generally stays behind, so the function generally remains asymmetric. It becomes an even function — with the x term vanishing too, leaving only "x⁴ + x², only even-degree terms" — only in the special case where a specific relationship holds among the coefficients (namely, that the extrema are equally spaced, i.e., that the derivative's center of symmetry lies on the x-axis). So a quartic function generally isn't even guaranteed to have y-axis symmetry, let alone point symmetry.

In other words, cubics always end up with "only odd-degree terms left," while quartics end up that way only under a special condition. This difference — whether removing one term always drags another term down with it, or only does so when an extra condition among the coefficients is met — is the real reason cubic and quartic functions are symmetric in different ways. (The concrete substitution calculation is worked out directly in the proof in Chapter 1 below.)

In short, a cubic function is "the simplest nonlinear function, and the first example where symmetry and nonlinearity coexist." This position is exactly what makes cubic functions special, both on exams and in mathematics as a whole.

2. The Big Picture: Two Perspectives

The point symmetry of cubic functions can be proven from two completely different sets of ingredients. What matters is that arriving at the same conclusion by two entirely different routes is itself evidence that "this symmetry isn't a coincidence — it's baked deep into the structure."

Perspective 1: The Algebraic View — Erasing the x² Term

For a general cubic f(x) = ax³ + bx² + cx + d, an appropriate translation can eliminate the x² term entirely. The function then takes the form "odd function + constant." An odd function is symmetric about the origin, and adding a constant just shifts the center of symmetry upward without destroying the symmetry itself.

Perspective 2: The Differential View — The Derivative Is a Parabola

The derivative f'(x) = 3ax² + 2bx + c is a quadratic function — a parabola. A parabola is always left-right symmetric about its own vertex (axis of symmetry). And the x-coordinate of that vertex is −b/(3a), which is exactly the x-coordinate of the inflection point.

How the two perspectives relate: Perspective 1 is an algebraic approach — "shift the function itself to the origin to make it an odd function." Perspective 2 is a differential approach — "start from the symmetry of the derivative." Two completely different sets of ingredients arrive at the same place.

3. What Follows From Symmetry

Point symmetry is beautiful on its own, but the real reason it's powerful on exams is that a huge number of proportional relationships derive from it. Symmetry is the seed; the proportional relationships are the stems that grow out of it.

(1) Symmetry of function values

Let the center of symmetry be x₀. Then the following always holds.

f(x₀ − t) + f(x₀ + t) = 2f(x₀)

If a problem gives you a condition like f(p − q) = f(p + q), that's a signal that "the function has an inflection point at x = p."

(2) Symmetry of the derivative's values

f'(x₀ − t) = f'(x₀ + t)

(3) Symmetry of the extrema

x₁ + x₂ = 2x₀

This is the starting point for every proportional-relationship problem. From here, the "1:1:1 trisection," the "1:√3 ratio," and the "5 equally spaced points" all follow.

(4) Symmetry of integrals

Integrals over intervals symmetric about the inflection point simplify dramatically. In area problems, this property cuts the computation down enormously.

4. A Map of the Proportional Relationships That Follow

Let's sketch, in advance, what stems grow out of the seed of symmetry.

① The 1:1:1 trisection

For a cubic with extrema, the four important x-coordinates adjacent to the inflection point form an arithmetic sequence.

−2k, −k, 0, k (with the inflection point at the origin)

② The 1:√3 ratio

Between the x-coordinate of the point where the horizontal line through the inflection point meets the cubic's graph, and the x-coordinate of the extremum, there's a 1:√3 relationship.

③ The 2:1 relationship via tangent lines

Measured from the inflection point, the distance to where a horizontal tangent through an extremum meets the curve again is twice the distance between the extremum and the inflection point. (That second point is not a point of tangency — just a simple intersection.)

④ 5 equally spaced points (the 1:2 extension)

−2k, −k, 0, k, 2k

⑤ The area formula

S = |a|/12 × |β − α|⁴

⑥ The formula for the difference in height between the extrema

Difference of extrema = |a| · d³ / 2

5. Why This Is Powerful on Exams

This symmetry and these proportional relationships are decisive on exams for three reasons.

First, the amount of computation drops dramatically. In a complicated extremum problem, setting up your unknowns directly with the 1:1:1 ratio wraps things up in a few lines, without solving a system of equations.

Second, it demands an integrated grasp of several concepts at once. Differentiation, integration, and the relationship between roots and coefficients are all tangled together in a single problem, making it a strong discriminator.

Third, the approach is highly standardized. Whenever a symmetry signal shows up in the problem's conditions, nearly every type can be solved with a single procedure below.

See a symmetry condition → move the inflection point to the origin → arrange as an odd function → factor

Now let's reuse one specific cubic function from start to finish, checking each property with numbers first and then proving why.

0. One Running Example We'll Keep Using

We'll base everything on the following function.

f(x) = x³ − 6x² + 9x + 2
  • At x = 0, f(0) = 2
  • At x = 1, f(1) = 6 ← local maximum
  • At x = 2, f(2) = 4 ← inflection point
  • At x = 3, f(3) = 2 ← local minimum

x = 0, 1, 2, 3 are equally spaced (spacing 1), and the values are 2, 6, 4, 2 — keep those numbers in mind, since they'll keep coming back later.

1. Every Cubic Function Is Symmetric

Checking with our eyes first

Look at the two points 1 unit away from the inflection point x = 2, on either side.

  • f(2 − 1) = f(1) = 6
  • f(2 + 1) = f(3) = 2
  • Average: (6 + 2) / 2 = 4 = f(2)

f(2 − t) + f(2 + t) = 2 × f(2) — this relationship holds for every t. This is precisely "point symmetry."

Proof 1: The algebraic proof — erasing the x² term

Substituting x = t − m (where m = b/(3a)) into f(x) = ax³ + bx² + cx + d, the coefficients by degree in t are:

Coefficient of t³: a Coefficient of t²: −3am + b Coefficient of t¹: 3am² − 2bm + c Coefficient of t⁰: −am³ + bm² − cm + d

Checking the t² coefficient: −3am + b = −3a·(b/3a) + b = −b + b = 0. It comes out to exactly 0 symbolically too — this substitution makes the t² term vanish for every cubic function.

After substitution we get g(t) = a·t³ + p·t + q, and since g(−t) = −at³ − pt + q, we have g(t) + g(−t) = 2q. This means the graph of g(t) is point-symmetric about the point (0, q).

Proof 2: The differential proof — using the symmetry of the derivative (a parabola)

f'(x) = 3ax² + 2bx + c is a parabola, whose vertex x-coordinate −b/(3a) is exactly the x-coordinate of the inflection point.

Letting x₀ = −b/(3a), we get f'(x₀+t) − f'(x₀−t) = 4t(3a·x₀ + b) = 4t(−b+b) = 0, which holds for all a, b, c, t.

Defining k(t) = f(x₀+t) + f(x₀−t) − 2f(x₀), we get k'(t) = f'(x₀+t) − f'(x₀−t) = 0, so k(t) is constant, and since k(0)=0, k(t) is always 0 — arriving at the same conclusion as Proof 1.

2. The "1:1:1" Trisection

Checking with our eyes first

x = 0, 1, 2, 3 are all spaced 1 apart. x = 0 → f(0) = 2, x = 3 → f(3) = 2 (the local minimum). There's a point, exactly at an equally-spaced position, whose value matches an extremum's value.

Why it happens (proof)

g(t) = at³ + pt, extrema at t=±k. Solving g(t)=g(k) gives (t−k)[a(t²+tk+k²)+p]=0; substituting the extremum condition p=−3ak² gives t²+tk−2k²=0 → (t−k)(t+2k)=0.

So we get the solution t = −2k, and the x-coordinates of the four points are −2k, −k, 0, k — spaced exactly k apart. (Combining the original factor (t−k) with the one just found, the full factorization is (t−k)²(t+2k)=0 — t=k being a double root makes sense since it's "the point whose value equals its own," so the only new root is t=−2k.)

3. "1 : √3"

Checking with our eyes first

Finding where the horizontal line y=4 through the inflection point (x=2, y=4) meets the curve gives x = 2, 2−√3, 2+√3. The distance to the extremum is 1, and the distance to the intersection is √3 — exactly a 1:√3 ratio.

Why it happens (proof)

g(t)=at³−3ak²t=0 → t=0, t=±√3k. Extremum condition: t²=k²; intersection condition: t²=3k². Since the ratio of t² values is 1:3, the ratio of t itself is 1:√3.

4. The Area Formula — Finding the Area Without Computation

(The relationship here — "point of tangency x=1, re-intersection x=4" — is the map's ③ 2:1 relationship via tangent lines. Measured from the inflection point (x=2), the distance to the re-intersection is 2, and to the extremum is 1 — exactly 2:1.)

Checking with our eyes first

The tangent y=6 at the local maximum (x=1, y=6) meets the curve at x=1 (point of tangency) and x=4 (intersection).

S = |a| / 12 × |β − α|⁴ = 1/12 × 3⁴ = 81/12 = 6.75

Full proof — actually carrying out the integral

Substituting u = x−α with d=β−α into ∫[α→β] a(x−α)²(x−β) dx:

= a ∫[0→d] (u³ − du²) du = a(d⁴/4 − d⁴/3) = −a·d⁴/12

Taking the absolute value for area: S = |a|/12 × |β−α|⁴. This holds for any α, β, a.

5. The 1:√3 Extension — Up to 5 Equally Spaced Points

Checking with our eyes first

The horizontal tangent through the local minimum (x=3, y=2) meets the curve again at x=0. Altogether, x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 are five points, all spaced 1 apart.

In the end, applying the 1:1:1 relationship on both sides naturally produces a structure of 5 equally-spaced points. It's not a new principle — just the same principle applied on both sides.

6. The Extrema-Difference Formula — Finding the Height Without Computation

Checking with our eyes first

Local maximum f(1)=6, local minimum f(3)=2, difference 4. Distance between extrema is 2.

Difference of extrema = |a| × (distance between extrema)³ / 2 = |1| × 2³ / 2 = 4

Why it happens (proof)

For g(t)=at³+pt, g(k)=−g(−k) (since g is odd). Which one is the max vs. min depends on the sign of a, but looking only at the absolute difference: |g(k)−g(−k)| = |2ak³+2pk|. Substituting the extremum condition p=−3ak² gives |−4ak³| = 4|a|k³. With distance d=2k, k=d/2, this becomes 4|a|(d/2)³ = |a|·d³/2.

8. Summary

Summarizing the properties we've seen, one line each:

  • Point symmetry: Occurs necessarily because eliminating the x² term by translation gives an odd function + constant
  • 1:1:1: Occurs because the cubic equation g(t)=g(k) factors as (t−k)(t+2k)
  • 1:√3: Occurs because the squared ratio of the extremum condition (t²=k²) to the condition for intersecting the horizontal line through the inflection point (t²=3k²) is 1:3
  • Area formula: Occurs because integrating the double-root structure (x−α)²(x−β) always simplifies to a fourth-power form
  • 5 equally spaced points: Occurs because applying the horizontal tangents at both extrema gives −2k,−k,0,k,2k
  • Extrema difference: Occurs because extrema come out as ±2ak³, and substituting d=2k simplifies it to |a|d³/2

All six of these derive from a single principle: "moving the inflection point to the origin turns the function into an odd function."
