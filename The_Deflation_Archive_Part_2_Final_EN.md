# The Deflation Archive — Part 2

*Deflation: Definition, the Core Puzzle, Candidate Mechanisms, Cases*

## 2-0. What this part does

This series asks only one question: what is inflation? Part 1 looked at the extreme in which money melts away at tens to thousands of percent per month. Part 2 looks at the opposite extreme of the working definition (*inflation is a sustained change in the general price level*): the extreme in which the general price level falls.

The habits are the same as in Part 1.

- We do not judge good or bad first. How deflation reaches whom depends on who holds how many nominal contracts, and that is a result to be found, not an assumption.
- We separate claims from what has been verified. Every figure carries its unit, index, and source.
- We do not fix a "true value." The goal is to explain why figures for the same event differ by index and period.

The risk is the same as well. Extreme cases magnify some causes and hide others. The 1930s do not by themselves explain an ordinary 1% decline.

If Part 1's hard question was "why do the figures differ?", Part 2's hard question is "why did the same fall in prices produce different outcomes in different episodes?" A second question follows: the size of "prices fell" often depends on which index the sentence refers to.


## 2-1. Definition

### 2-1.1 Three neighboring concepts

- **Disinflation**: the rate of price increase slows but is still positive. Prices keep rising.
- **Deflation**: a general price index falls. This is the subject of this part.
- **Relative price decline**: only specific items fall in price (e.g., electronics). This is not deflation.

### 2-1.2 Which prices?

Goods-and-services prices (consumer prices, wholesale prices, the GDP deflator) and asset prices (housing, equities) are different objects. The BIS reports that results differ depending on whether the two are separated (Borio·Erdem·Filardo·Hofmann 2015, pp.31–32, 37–42). The distinction matters for these reasons:

- A fall in goods-and-services prices is ex ante ambiguous in its effects. If supply grows, prices fall while income and output rise. If demand shrinks, prices, income, and output fall together (BIS 2015, p.31).
- A fall in asset prices is not ex ante ambiguous. It cuts wealth and collateral values and depresses demand and output (BIS 2015, p.32). How strong the effect is, however, is an empirical question.
- The two kinds of declines often occur together. So the costs of asset price declines can be mistaken for the costs of goods price declines (BIS 2015, p.32).

### 2-1.3 The BIS definition and the peak rule

- The BIS defines deflation simply as "a fall in the price index in question." This choice avoids the difficulty of separating one-off relative price adjustments from self-reinforcing changes, and the problem of building costs into the definition in advance (BIS 2015, p.32).
- Instead, it classifies as *persistent* deflation a cumulative fall in the price level, measured by annual consumer prices, lasting five years or more, and calls the rest *transitory* deflation (BIS 2015, p.33). A cumulative decline is used in order to span periods when the index was highly volatile, particularly under the classical gold standard.
- Peak identification has three steps (BIS 2015, p.33, footnote 3): (i) find candidate peaks using a five-year moving average; (ii) pin down the local peak by date in the raw series; (iii) the peak must be higher than the price level in the five years before and after. The symmetry condition (iii) keeps persistent deflation episodes from overlapping. The list of peaks is in Appendix Table A3.
- Footnote 6 gives a concrete case. The procedure identifies most of the 1920s as a period of persistent deflation in many countries; in some countries there was price stability or a temporary weak rise in the mid-1920s, but it was not long or strong enough to count as a separate peak. In particular, for the United States, the weak rise in prices in 1923–26 that followed the sharp deflation of the early 1920s is not treated as a separate peak (BIS 2015, p.35, footnote 6).
- Sample: 38 countries, 1870–2013, with start years differing by country (BIS 2015, Table A2). Regimes are divided into the classical gold standard (1870–1913), the interwar period (1919–38), and the postwar period (1946–2013) (BIS 2015, p.33).

### 2-1.4 Good deflation and bad deflation

- There is a convention of distinguishing by cause. If prices fall because supply grows faster than demand (productivity, competition, cheaper inputs), it is the good kind; if they fall because demand shrinks faster than supply, it is the bad kind (Bordo·Landon Lane·Redish, NBER WP 10329; BIS 2015, p.31).
- The abstract of Bordo·Redish (NBER WP 9520) itself warns that this simple distinction does not capture the complex experience before 1896.
- The BIS notes that the relatively good output record of the classical gold standard is why such studies called that period "good" deflation (BIS 2015, p.37). It adds that postwar deflations are also on the "good" side by the same criterion.
- This classification is a starting point, not a conclusion. The conclusion of this part is that "which side the cause was on at the time, and how output actually changed, must be examined separately."
- Whether deflation was anticipated is also an axis. The abstract of BIS WP 152 (Borio·Filardo 2004) says most deflations were largely unanticipated. Footnote 12, however, adds the qualification that deflation undertaken to restore convertibility after a war was more likely to have been anticipated.

### 2-1.5 How the definition changes the result

- A–K divide episodes into five-year blocks and define "average inflation < 0" as deflation and "average real growth < 0" as depression (Atkeson·Kehoe 2004, NBER WP 10268).
- The BIS pins down the price peak by date and looks at the difference in real per-capita GDP growth over the five years before and after it (BIS 2015, Graph 3).
- The two approaches can give different answers for the same event. A–K's Great Depression episode (1929–34) is by definition an "episode with deflation," while the BIS's interwar peak comparison is centered on peaks in 1925–31 (see 2-2.5).
- We do not read a difference in definitions as evidence that one side is "wrong." The basic stance of this part is "state the definition alongside the figure."

## 2-2. The core puzzle: why is the link weak outside the Great Depression?

### 2-2.1 Atkeson & Kehoe (2004)

Source: Atkeson, A. & Kehoe, P., *Deflation and Depression: Is There an Empirical Link?*, NBER WP 10268; AER Papers and Proceedings 94(2), 2004.

Design:

- Data from 17 countries (start dates by country 1820–1908, ending in 2000) are divided into **five-year episodes**.
- An episode is deflation if its average inflation rate is negative, and depression if its average real growth rate is negative.
- Only moderate inflation, below 20% a year, is included.

Results:

- **The Great Depression episode (1929–34)**: all 16 countries had deflation, and 8 of them had depression. The slope from regressing output growth on inflation is 0.40 (standard error 0.28). The authors themselves do not call this an "overwhelmingly tight link." On the strength of the link, they note that Eichengreen·Sachs and Bernanke·Carey see it as tight, while Cole·Ohanian·Leung see the opposite.
- **Outside the Great Depression**: of 73 deflation episodes, 65 (about 90%) had no depression. The slopes are 0.04 including wars, 0.10 excluding war-related episodes, 0.11 prewar (before 1939), and −0.03 postwar (after 1949). In the postwar period there are no five-year-average deflation episodes at all.
- **More depressions without deflation**: of 29 depressions, 21 (about 72%) had no deflation, and 10 of those were related to the world wars. The same passage of the original text also contains the sentence "8 of 29 had no deflation," but by arithmetic it appears to be a typo. Since 73 deflation episodes = 8 with depression + 65 without, and 29 depressions = 8 with deflation + 21 without, "21" is correct (calculated value).
- **Japan**: growth rates in the 1990s were Japan 1.41, US 3.20, Italy 1.61, France 1.84. The authors consider it reasonable to see Japan's growth slowdown as the fading of catch-up growth rather than as deflation.

Limitations the authors themselves state:

- It is a "raw-data correlation" that does not control for whether deflation was anticipated or for the monetary regime.
- Theory distinguishes anticipated from unanticipated deflation. The authors point out that if the Great Depression deflation was unanticipated, it has no bearing on evaluating the costs of anticipated deflation, but they do not test this.
- The data end in 2000.

### 2-2.2 BIS (2015): 140 years of deflation records

Source: Borio, C., Erdem, M., Filardo, A. & Hofmann, B., *The costs of deflations: a historical perspective*, BIS Quarterly Review, March 2015, pp.31–49.

Record (Table 1):

- 663 deflation years and 3,024 inflation years. The share of deflation years is about 18% (calculated value).
- Classical gold standard (1870–1913): 294 deflation, 368 inflation → deflation share 44% (calculated value).
- Interwar (1920–38): 240 deflation, 282 inflation → share 46% (calculated value).
- Postwar (1947–2013): 129 deflation, 2,374 inflation → share 5% (calculated value).
- 66 persistent deflations: classical gold standard 33, interwar 29, postwar 4. The four postwar cases are Japan (twice, with price peaks in 1998 and 2008), China, and Hong Kong (BIS 2015, p.34). The sub-periods (1920–28 with 22, 1929–38 with 26, 1930–33 with 26) overlap with the interwar period and are not summed.
- Average rate of decline in persistent deflations: overall −3.0%, classical gold standard −2.5%, interwar −4.0%, Great Depression (1930–33) −5.1%, postwar −0.6%. Average duration: overall 7.4 years, classical gold standard 6.8 years, interwar 8.5 years, Great Depression 3.3 years, postwar 4.7 years.
- Average rate of decline in all deflations (including transitory): overall −3.9%, classical gold standard −3.8%, interwar −5.0%, Great Depression −5.4%, postwar −1.9%. The Great Depression's −5.4% is only slightly deeper than the interwar average of −5.0%. This agrees with the BIS judgment (p.35) that the Great Depression is not an outlier in terms of average or cumulative price decline.

Growth (Table 2, real per-capita GDP, deflation years versus inflation years):

- Overall 1.5% versus 2.7% (difference 1.2 pp, significant at 1%).
- Classical gold standard 1.5% versus 1.6% (difference 0.1 pp, not significant).
- Interwar 0.5% versus 3.5% (difference 3.0 pp, significant at 1%). 1920–28 is 2.3% versus 3.5% (difference 1.2 pp, significant at 10%), 1929–38 is −0.8% versus 3.5% (difference 4.3 pp, significant at 1%), and the Great Depression 1930–33 is −2.2% versus 0.8% (difference 3.0 pp).
- Postwar 3.2% versus 2.7% (difference −0.5 pp, not significant). In the postwar period, growth in deflation years is actually higher.
- Average growth during persistent deflations: overall 1.0%, classical gold standard 1.3%, interwar 0.5%, 1929–38 −1.7%, Great Depression −3.3%, postwar 2.1%.

Five-year comparison after the price peak (Graph 3, average growth in the five years after the peak − average growth in the five years before):

- Overall: 0.8 − 2.1 = −1.3 pp (significant at 1%, 48 peaks).
- Classical gold standard: 1.1 − 1.7 = −0.6 pp (not significant, 32 peaks).
- Interwar: −0.6 − 3.0 = −3.6 pp (significant at 1%, 12 peaks). Only in the interwar period did output actually fall (BIS 2015, p.37).
- Postwar: 2.9 − 2.6 = +0.3 pp (not significant, 4 peaks). Because the sample is only 4 cases, the BIS says to take it "with a grain of salt" (footnote 7).
- Note: the BIS itself writes that "the link is weak," but the −1.3 pp for the full sample is statistically significant. "Weak" means not "zero" but "the interwar period drives it."

### 2-2.3 Adding asset prices changes the picture

The BIS (2015, pp.37–42) also analyzes housing and equity price peaks in the same framework.

- **Five years after housing price peaks (Graph 4)**: overall 0.5 − 2.4 = −1.9 pp (significant at 1%, 58 peaks). Classical gold standard 1 − 1.5 = −0.5 pp (not significant, 16). Interwar 0.4 − 2.2 = −1.8 pp (significant at 10%, 15). Postwar 0.3 − 3.1 = −2.8 pp (significant at 1%, 27).
- **Five years after equity price peaks (Graph 5)**: overall 0.9 − 2.9 = −2.0 pp (significant at 1%, 111 peaks). Classical gold standard 0.7 − 1.6 = −0.9 pp (significant at 5%, 25). Interwar −1.7 − 3.3 = −5.0 pp (significant at 1%, 13). Postwar 1.5 − 3.4 = −1.9 pp (significant at 1%, 73).
- **Regression including all three kinds of peaks at once (Annex Table A1, growth in the h years after the peak − growth in the h years before)**: in the full sample, the CPI peak coefficients for h=1 to 5 are 0.01, 0.01, −0.01, 0.00, 0.01, all insignificant. Housing peaks are −0.02\*\*, −0.06\*\*\*, −0.08\*\*\*, −0.10\*\*\*, −0.11\*\*\*, and equity peaks are −0.02\*\*\*, −0.06\*\*\*, −0.07\*\*\*, −0.09\*\*\*, −0.09\*\*\*. That is, growth is lower by about 10 pp cumulatively over five years (BIS 2015, p.42).
- **Interwar only**: CPI peak coefficients −0.01, −0.02, −0.05, −0.04, −0.01 (all insignificant). Housing −0.01, −0.06, −0.10\*\*\*, −0.12\*\*, −0.11\*\*. Equities −0.02\*, −0.09\*\*\*, −0.17\*\*\*, −0.25\*\*\*, −0.23\*\*\*.
- **Entering CPI peaks alone** (Memo): for the interwar period the coefficients are much larger at −0.02, −0.04\*\*, −0.09, −0.12, −0.07. That they are large yet mostly insignificant may be because the small sample gives low statistical power (inference). This should be read not as "no effect" but as "the estimate is uncertain, and looks large when asset prices are left out." The BIS also points out that the coefficients become smaller when measured as changes relative to the country average (p.42, footnote 15).
- **A postwar exception at a one-year lag**: the CPI peak is +0.03\*\* at h=1, a sign meaning deflation brings stronger growth.

### 2-2.4 Is debt the problem? BIS 2015 Table 4

- Table 4 (full sample, difference in cumulative growth over five years after the peak): housing price peak × private credit gap −0.27\*\*\*. CPI peak × debt is 0.09 for the public debt ratio, 0.16 for the private debt ratio, and 0.62 for the credit gap, all insignificant. Equity peak × debt is −0.04 (private) and −0.22 (credit gap), insignificant.
- That is, there are traces that high private debt amplified the shock of housing price deflation, but no traces that it amplified the shock of goods-and-services price deflation (BIS 2015, p.46).
- **Comparing the size of asset losses** (BIS 2015, pp.46–47): in the 2008 US crisis, housing lost about $9.1 trillion in value (Case–Shiller basis) and equities about $11.3 trillion (S&P 500 basis). By contrast, if 1% deflation continues for three years, the increase in real debt is about $1.1 trillion (about $0.4 trillion for households, about $0.35 trillion each for non-financial firms and government). Footnote 23 says that even under the extreme assumptions that expected inflation is 2% and all debt is fixed-rate, the loss is only about three times as large.
- **A difference in nature**: the BIS explains that an asset price decline is a fall in (at least perceived) net wealth, while a goods price decline is mainly a redistribution from debtors to creditors (p.47).
- **Limitations**: in the interwar period there are only 5 CPI peaks, 10 housing, and 12 equity, so the regression sample is very small (p.46). The BIS itself says the debt analysis is "exploratory." Because housing price data get worse in quality the further back one goes, the results may have underestimated the importance of housing (p.37, footnote 8).

### 2-2.5 Differences in the peak rule between BIS 2004 (WP 152) and 2015

- 2004 edition (Borio·Filardo, BIS WP 152, March 2004): candidate peaks are found with a five-year moving average of CPI, and the highest value of the unsmoothed raw series within a five-year window around the candidate is taken as the peak. The condition "higher than the five years before and after" does not appear in the text or footnotes.
- The 2004 edition gives 1926 as the US price peak just before the Great Depression. Table 9's US row lists 1837, 1847, 1857, 1866, 1881, 1891, 1920, and 1926, and the five observations in the interwar 1925–39 sample in Table 10, footnote 2, are the US (1926), France (1930), Italy (1926), Canada (1929), and Germany (1928).
- The 2015 edition adds (iii) (higher than the five years before and after) to the same three steps, and footnote 6 states directly that the weak price rise of 1923–26 in the United States is not treated as a separate peak. Accordingly, the US CPI peaks in the 2015 edition's Table A3 are 1882, 1891, and 1920.
- Conclusion: the change in the US peak from 1926 (2004) to 1920 (2015) is not a conflict in the data but a difference in the peak rule (2015 footnote 6 is the basis). The note to the 2015 Graph 3 excludes five-year trajectories that include observations from 1914–18 and 1939–45. The 1920 peak is excluded because its preceding five years overlap with World War I years, so the Great Depression in the US likely does not enter the 2015 edition's 12 interwar peaks as a CPI peak event (inference). The number of 1925–31 peak candidates in Table A3 is 13 and the number the BIS reports is 12, so we have not been able to verify which one is missing.
- The "excluding peaks in 1919–20" wording of the 2004 edition is not in the note to Table 10 but in Table 12's "Full sample (excluding peaks in 1919–20)" and footnote 4 (p.41), where the crisis variable coefficient is 3.06 (standard error 1.44) when included and 1.79 (standard error 1.67) when excluded.

### 2-2.6 Interim conclusion

- A fall in prices by itself is a weak predictor of depression. Atkeson·Kehoe (65 of 73 deflations outside the Great Depression had no depression) and the BIS (growth is higher in postwar deflation years) both point this way.
- So the puzzle is not "why is deflation bad?" but "what coincided in the 1930s?"
- However, the definitions in the two studies differ (A–K use five-year average negative growth; the BIS uses the difference in growth in the five years around the peak). The samples are also small. The BIS itself writes that there are only four postwar persistent deflations.

## 2-3. Candidate mechanisms: claim cards

A claim card is a list that sets side by side who claims what, using what data, together with the counterarguments. It is not a ranking.

### Card C-A. Debt deflation

- **Claim**: A fall in prices after excessive debt is the core of the great depression.
- **Proponent and evidence**: Fisher, I. (1933), *The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions*, Econometrica 1(4), 337–357.
  - By March 1933, debt had been liquidated by about 20%, but the value of money (in terms of wholesale commodity prices) had grown by about 75%, so real debt had grown by about 40%. (0.8) × (1.75) = 1.40 (calculated value). A 1.75-fold rise in the value of money means that wholesale prices fell by about 43% (1/1.75 ≈ 0.571, calculated value).
  - He condenses this situation as "The more the debtors pay, the more they owe."
  - The path is debt liquidation → contraction of deposit currency → fall in prices → decline in net worth → decline in profits and output. He regards debt without deflation, or deflation without excessive debt, as much weaker (clauses 29–30).
- **Counterarguments and limitations**:
  - Fisher himself says his conclusions are tentative.
  - BIS (2015, footnote 16): Fisher's path is originally a process in which debt liquidation reduces deposit currency and lowers prices, and empirical evidence for debt deflation is "scant and anecdotal." Bernanke·James (1991) did not include it explicitly, Fackler·Parker (2005) inferred it from the rise in debt in the 1920s, Eichengreen·Mitchener (2003) call the Great Depression a "credit boom gone wrong," and Meltzer (2003) objects that falling prices should raise real balances and stimulate spending.
  - BIS Table 4: goods price peak × debt is insignificant (2-2.4).

### Card C-B. Real interest rates and the zero lower bound

- **Claim**: When the nominal interest rate hits zero, deflation raises the real interest rate and blocks room for monetary policy.
- **Evidence**: one of the links in Fisher's (1933) chain (nominal rates fall while real rates rise). Bernanke (2002), *Deflation: Making Sure "It" Doesn't Happen Here*. BIS WP 152 summarizes that the zero lower bound was historically binding only rarely, in the interwar period (Table 13, p.42). Nakaso (2014): Japan's policy rate fell from 6% in August 1990 to 0.25% in September 1998, so conventional tools were nearly exhausted.
- **Counterargument**: Bank of Canada SWP 2017-16 (*Downward Nominal Wage Rigidity Meets the Zero Lower Bound*) shows by model that downward nominal wage rigidity reduces how often the zero lower bound is hit and also eases the severity of recessions, weakening the case for raising the inflation target.

### Card C-C. Collapse of credit intermediation

- **Claim**: The financial collapse raised the cost of credit and suppressed demand.
- **Evidence**:
  - Bernanke, B. (1983), *Nonmonetary Effects of the Financial Crisis in the Propagation of the Great Depression*, AER 73(3), 257–276.
  - Calomiris & Mason (2003a), AER 93(3), 937–947: explaining state-level income growth (1930–32, Table 2, p.941) with loan supply (instrumented), a one-standard-deviation (17.9%) decline in loan supply growth at the sample mean leads to about a 7% decline in income over the same period, and about 9% when deposits are used as the instrument (pp.943–944). At the county level, a one-standard-deviation (23.4%) deposit decline is associated with a 67.7% decline in the value of building permits (p.944).
- **Counterargument**: Calomiris & Mason (2003b), *Fundamentals, Panics, and Bank Distress During the Depression*, AER 93(5), 1615–1647. The abstract summarizes that bank failure risk is well explained by fundamentals, that the first and second Friedman–Schwartz crises are not associated with unexplained positive residual risk, that the third is ambiguous but with a small aggregate effect, and that only in early 1933 is there a large unexplained increase. Figure 4 (AER p.1635): the predicted mean survival time of Fed member banks, without the F–S crisis indicators, rises from about 200 days in January 1930 to about 1,300 days in early 1933, and with the January and February 1933 indicators included it falls to about 700 days at the same time. That difference of about 600 days is the upper bound of the panic effect (read from the figure scale, ±50 days).

### Card C-D. Asset price deflation and private debt

- **Claim**: More than a fall in goods prices, a fall in asset prices, especially housing prices, is harmful when it meets private debt.
- **Evidence**: the figures in BIS (2015), 2-2.3 and 2-2.4. Cumulative growth over the five years after the peak was about 10 pp lower for both housing and equities, and the coefficient on housing price peak × private credit gap is −0.27 (significant at 1%). As a follow-up interpretation, the BIS (2015, conclusion p.48) writes that the size of the output loss in the Great Depression may be more related to the asset price crash and banking problems than to the fall in prices itself. BIS WP 152 (2004) likewise sees asset price declines as a larger explanation of Japan's output stagnation than mild deflation.
- **Counterarguments and limitations**: the sample is small (four postwar persistent deflations, five interwar CPI peaks) and the debt analysis is exploratory. Housing price data get worse in quality the further back one goes (BIS 2015, footnote 8). Fisher's path (goods prices) and the BIS path (asset prices) are in tension, and this document draws no conclusion. WP 152 footnote 27 cites Fukao (2003) to introduce the view that the asset price decline itself reflects expectations of falling prices.

### Card C-E. Nominal wage rigidity

- **Claim**: Wages could not fall as fast as prices, so real wages rose and output fell.
- **Evidence**:
  - Bernanke & Carey (1996), *Nominal Wage Stickiness and Aggregate Supply in the Great Depression*, QJE 111(3), 853–883; NBER WP 5439: 22 countries (Argentina, Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Estonia, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Japan, Latvia, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Poland, Sweden, Switzerland, the UK, the US). Levels sample 1931–36, differences sample 1933–36 (Table II). The dependent variable is industrial production; the nominal wage coefficient is negative (levels specification −1.423, t 2.21; differences specification −1.049, t 1.74) and the price coefficient is positive (1.581; 0.890). That is, a rise in nominal wages is associated with a fall in output, and a rise in prices with a rise in output.
  - Mishkin (2008, Fed speech, March 27, 2008): downward nominal wage rigidity is one reason central banks set a target slightly above 0%; with about 1% or more, the frequency of hitting the zero lower bound falls sharply, and at about 2% it is hardly constraining.
- **Counterarguments and limitations**:
  - Cole·Ohanian·Leung (2005), *Deflation and the International Great Depression: A Productivity Puzzle*, NBER WP 11237 = Minneapolis Fed Staff Report 356: in 17 countries in 1929–33, about 2/3 of the change in output is explained by productivity shocks and about 1/3 by monetary (deflation) shocks, because there is no systematic relationship between deflation and output. The only variable highly correlated with the productivity shock is stock prices. Table 2 (p.46, cumulative log change 1929→1932): US output −0.30 and deflator −0.27; Italy output −0.08 and deflator −0.24; average output −0.10 and deflator −0.16 (text p.6: average price decline 16%). Converting the log changes to simple percentages gives US prices about −24% and output about −26%, and Italy prices about −21% and output about −8% (calculated value). The point is that output losses differed greatly for similar price declines.
  - BIS (2015, Box 1): US manufacturing hourly wages fell by about 1/5 in the Great Depression. The problem may not be that wages did not fall but that they did not fall as fast as prices (interpretation).
  - Mishkin himself cites Swiss and Japanese evidence that downward wage rigidity shows up less in deflation or very low inflation.
  - WP 152 footnote 34: non-wage channels may have been at work.

### Card C-F. The gold standard

- **Claim**: The gold standard transmitted and deepened deflation and depression internationally.
- **Evidence**:
  - Eichengreen, B. & Sachs, J. (1985), *Exchange Rates and Economic Recovery in the 1930s*, NBER WP 1498; Choudhri & Kochin (1980): countries that left the gold standard early recovered first.
  - Bernanke, B. (2004), *Money, Gold, and the Great Depression*, Federal Reserve Board, March 2, 2004: under the gold standard countries had to match tightening abroad, and the longer a country stayed on gold, the deeper its depression and the slower its recovery. Britain left in September 1931 and the US in March 1933, and he cites Friedman–Schwartz that China, on a silver standard, largely escaped the Great Depression.
  - Ellison, M., Lee, S. S. & O'Rourke, K. H. (2020), *The Ends of 27 Big Depressions*, CEPR DP15061 / NBER WP 27586; AER 114(1), 2024: the argument that in 27 countries, leaving the gold standard raised expected inflation and lowered real interest rates, contributing to recovery. Among the 15 Group A countries with clear departure dates (Belgium, British India, Denmark, Dutch East Indies, Finland, France, Japan, the Netherlands, Peru, Sweden, Switzerland, the UK, Canada, South Africa, New Zealand; pp.22–23), in 9 (Belgium, British India, Canada, Denmark, New Zealand, Peru, South Africa, Switzerland, the UK) the departure coincides with the turning point in expectations (p.23, footnote 31). Italy is not in Group A.
  - Fisher (1933) also holds, in a footnote, that the 1929 price level cannot be maintained under the gold standard.
- **Counterarguments and limitations**: 1873–96 under the same gold standard was largely favorable (Bordo et al.). So it may be a combination of credibility, banks, and debt rather than the regime itself (interpretation). BIS (Box 1): the median output change across 15 countries in the Great Depression is −7%, with large dispersion by country. Friedman·Schwartz (1963, p.41) treat the US in 1865–79 as a deflation in the period of preparation for returning to gold (1879), yet interpret rising output as having accompanied and caused it (see 2-4.1).

### Card C-G. Wage and cartel policy during the recovery

- **Claim**: The recovery after 1933 was weak because New Deal policies (NIRA, etc.) held wages and prices above trend.
- **Evidence**: Cole, H. & Ohanian, L. (2004), *New Deal Policies and the Persistence of the Great Depression: A General Equilibrium Analysis*, JPE 112(4). A UCLA press release presents this factor as explaining "about 60% of the weak recovery" (secondary source).
- **Counterarguments and limitations**: It depends on the model. This hypothesis concerns not the *occurrence* of the 1929–33 deflation but the *delayed recovery* in 1933–39, and its direction is opposite to that of Card C-E (wages rose too much). The 60% is a figure from a press release summary.

### Card C-H. Deflationary equilibrium and entrenched expectations

- **Claim**: If deflation lasts long, expectations harden and the economy falls into a vicious circle of prices↓ → sales and profits↓ → stagnant wages → weak consumption ("deflationary equilibrium"), from which escape is difficult.
- **Evidence**: Nakaso, H. (2014), *Japan's Economy and Monetary Policy*, Bank of Japan speech, July 8, 2014. He says the practice of raising base pay disappeared for a time. Even so, he judges that the "deflationary vicious circle" was avoided, since real GDP grew at an average annual rate of 1.5% in 2000–07. He names a weak price stability commitment as the reason past policy fell short (the forward guidance criterion of the 2001–06 quantitative easing, CPI excluding fresh food at 0% or above, was too low). When QQE was introduced (April 2013), CPI excluding fresh food was −0.5%, and in May 2014 it is +1.4% (excluding the effect of the consumption tax increase).
- **Counterarguments and limitations**: It is a speech by a deputy governor of the central bank that introduced and defends QQE, so there is a stake. A–K say Japan's growth slowdown can be seen as the fading of catch-up growth, and the BIS says that once demographic effects are taken into account, per-capita growth in the 2000s actually improved (see 2-4.4).

### The common structure across the cards

- Most strong claims speak not of a single factor but of a **combination**: debt × deflation (Fisher), asset prices × debt (BIS), gold standard × banking crisis (Eichengreen·Sachs, Bernanke 2004).
- Fisher himself wrote that the cases with only debt or only deflation are much weaker (clauses 29–30). The BIS also draws its conclusion from interactions.
- One case can be a counterexample to another claim. The favorable gold standard experience of 1873–96 is a counterexample to the simple form of Card C-F, which is why it survives only in the form "regime × conditions."
- This document draws no conclusion on the tension between Card C-A (goods price path) and C-D (asset price path). On wages, too, the claim that "there was rigidity" (C-E) and the claim that "wages rose too much" (C-G) speak of the same variable from different directions in different periods.
- The Japanese type (C-H) and the 1929–33 type (C-C, C-F) are different profiles, and there is no basis for thinking that a single theory explains both.

## 2-4. Cases

### 2-4.1 The Great Deflation, c. 1865–96: mostly favorable, but poorly perceived

**Basic account.** Bordo·Landon Lane·Redish (NBER WP 10329) report that in the US, UK, and Germany this deflation reflected both positive supply shocks and negative money supply shocks, but that the monetary shocks had little effect on output (interpreted as the short-run supply curve being very steep). The conclusion is "mainly good deflation." The NBER Digest adds two things: the authors did not explicitly distinguish actual from expected price changes, and people did not perceive this deflation as good. Politics followed perception (the controversy over the "Crime of 1873" surrounding the 1873 Coinage Act, and Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech in 1896; per the Wikipedia entry).

The BIS (2015, Graph 3) finds that under the classical gold standard (1870–1913), growth in the five years after a price peak was only 0.6 pp lower than in the five years before, not significant (32 peaks), and so introduces prior studies that call this period "good" deflation (Bordo·Redish 2004, Atkeson·Kehoe 2004, Borio·Filardo 2004, Bordo·Filardo 2005) (p.37).

**US figures for 1869–79 diverge greatly by series.** All figures in the list below are annual averages.

- **Kuznets series**: real NNP +6.8%, nominal NNP +3.0%, implicit prices −3.8%, real per capita +4.5%, population +2.3%, money stock (greenback value) +2.7% (Friedman·Schwartz 1963, p.37, Table 2). The rates of change are continuously compounded. Corresponding figures: real NNP 7.36 → 14.52 (billions of 1929 dollars), nominal NNP 5.82 → 7.89, prices 79.1 → 54.3 (1929=100), money stock 1.298 → 1.698, velocity 4.48 → 4.65, population 39.1 → 49.2 (millions), per capita 188 → 295 (dollars). Arithmetic check: ln(14.52/7.36)/10 = 6.79%, ln(54.3/79.1)/10 = −3.76%, 5.82/1.298 = 4.48 (calculated value).
- **F–S's own alternative estimates** (F–S 1963, Table 3 p.39; pp.38, 40): by back-calculation from the monetary base, real NNP grows at 4.9% a year (rough estimate) or 4.3% (refined estimate), nominal NNP at 1.1% or 0.5%, and real per capita at 2.6% or 2.0%. The total change is Kuznets's nominal +35% and real +97% versus the money-based nominal +12% or +5% and real +63% or +54% (p.38). F–S see the money-based estimates as confirming Kuznets's conclusion of "unusually rapid growth," but with noticeably lower growth rates and possibly large errors (p.39). They cite the fact that growth is concentrated at "nearly 9% a year" in 1875→79 and velocity rose about 17% (p.40) as grounds for possible overstatement in the Kuznets series.
- **The estimate F–S adopted for January 1867–February 1879** (pp.32–34, 38): they adopt a market price decline of 3.5% a year as a reasonable estimate and, of the 4.6 pp gap between money +1.1% and prices −3.5%, attribute about 1 pp to the long-run decline in velocity and the remaining 3.6 pp to output growth, obtaining real income of 3.6% a year (total +54%).
- **Balke–Gordon (1989) Table 10** (JPE 97(1), pp.84–85): nominal GNP 8.21 → 9.41, real GNP 78.2 → 123.1 (billions of constant 1982 dollars), deflator 10.49 → 7.64. Annual real +4.54%, deflator −3.1%, nominal +1.4% (calculated value). Sensitive to endpoints: with an 1878 endpoint it is 3.8% a year, 1875→79 is 5.0% a year, and 1878→79 alone is +12.3%.
- **Romer (1989) Table 2** (JPE 97(1), pp.1–37): real GNP 75.609 → 127.675, deflator 10.244 → 7.378. Annual real +5.4%, prices −3.2% (calculated value). Romer holds that GNP tracks only about 0.58–0.61 times the fluctuation in commodity output, which reduces volatility, and explains that Gallman's revision raises the 1869 GNP level and so removes part of the high 1870s growth in the Kuznets series.
- **F–S 1982 stage data** (*Monetary Trends*, chapter 5 Table 5.7, stage midpoints 1870.0 → 1880.5): real income about +5.6% a year, prices about −2.0% a year, nominal income about +3.2% a year (calculated value). The interval is not the same as 1869–79 and the values are stage averages, so they are approximate.
- **Beware of wholesale price figures**: the "6½% a year" and "5.4% a year" often seen in writing about US deflation in 1865–79 are not real growth rates or NNP prices but rates of decline in **wholesale prices**. According to F–S 1963 p.32, wholesale prices fell 6½% a year in 1865–79 (14 years) (footnote 23: comparing the Warren–Pearson index's 1865 and first-quarter 1879 averages), and from January 1867 to February 1879 the charted wholesale price index fell 5.4% a year, while nominal money stock rose 1.3% a year over the same period. This document does not use these as NNP prices because F–S themselves consider this index exaggerated owing to its heavy weight on farm products and raw materials, and present alternative indexes with declines of 2.3–3.9% a year (Hoover consumer prices −3.1%, Snyder–Tucker −3.5%, New York Fed cost of living −2.1%, Warren–Pearson annual edition −4.9%) (pp.33–34).

**F–S's interpretation.** Under any estimate, an unusually rapid increase in output turned unusually slow money growth into a large fall in prices, and the 50% deflation over the 15 years after 1865 did not produce a recession but was accompanied, and indeed caused, by a rapid rise in real income. The chain of causation runs "from output expansion to price decline" (F–S 1963, pp.40–41).

**Caveat.** F–S 1982 (p.156, footnote 19) note that the US money stock in 1865–79 declined relative to trend (exact estimates are possible from 1869) and link this to the period of return to the gold standard at the prewar parity. So it is hard to read this deflation as "purely supply-driven." Meanwhile, by Anderson (2003, Table 3), F–S M2 went from 1.28 in 1869 to 1.66 in 1879 (+2.6% a year), and from 1.62 in 1873 to 1.66 in 1879 (about 0.4% a year), nearly stagnant in the late 1870s (calculated value). A caution on monetary definitions: the 1873–78 money stock of 1.660 in F–S 1982 Table 5.1 is before adjustment, while the 3.300 in Table 5.7 is the value after the "financial sophistication" adjustment (pp.150–151, about double).

**Summary of growth rates**: 6.8% (Kuznets), 4.3–4.9% (F–S, money-based), 3.6% (F–S's adopted figure for 1867–79.2), 4.5% (Balke–Gordon), 5.4% (Romer), 5.6% (F–S 1982 stage data). The direction is the same. While prices fell greatly, real growth was large, and its size changes from 3.6% to 6.8% depending on the series chosen. This range is itself an example of this document's habit of "not fixing a true value."

**Two points of caution in handling this.** (1) The UK figures for this period (wholesale about −39% versus GDP deflator about −20%, and a summary table of −2.2% a year in prices and +1.6% growth) rest on blogs and secondary summaries, so this document uses them only as an example of index dependence. We have not verified the values in Feinstein (1972) Table 61 (the GDP deflator table). (2) The IJEF 2025 paper (single author) that compiles employment and unemployment rates for this period (employment +33%, unemployment rate 3.97% → 6.59%) contains transcription errors from the Romer table and needs to be checked against the original data.

### 2-4.2 US 1920–21: sharp and short

**Estimates of the price decline** differ by source.

- Balke–Gordon about −13%, Romer −14.8%, Commerce Department −18%, wholesale prices −36.8% (per the Wikipedia-family entry, secondary source).
- The Romer (1989) Table 2 deflator goes from 17.746 in 1920 to 15.124 in 1921, −14.8% (calculated value).
- The Balke–Gordon Table 10 (JPE 97(1), p.85) deflator is 15.23 in 1919 → 17.58 in 1920 → 15.30 in 1921. 1919→20 is +15.4%, 1920→21 is −13.0%, 1919→21 is +0.5% (calculated value). The 1920 figure of 17.58 is the highest in 1919–23. After that it is 14.22 in 1922, 14.63 in 1923, and 14.64 in 1929.
- Pieces introducing Grant (*The Forgotten Depression: 1921, the Crash That Cured Itself*, 2014) cite producer prices −40.8%, industrial production −31.6%, stock prices −46.6%, corporate profits −92%, and farm income −56.7% (Coolidge Review excerpt, secondary source). This account rests on advocacy-leaning sources (Grant is the publisher of an interest-rate newsletter, and the reviews are from Cato, the Mises Institute, etc.), so the figures and the claims should be read separately.

**Estimates of output** also diverge greatly by series.

- Romer (1989) Table 2: real GNP (billions of constant 1982 dollars) 1919 503.873 → 1920 498.132 → 1921 486.377. 1920→21 −2.4%, 1919→21 −3.5% (calculated value).
- Balke–Gordon Table 10: nominal GNP 1919 77.22 → 1920 87.24 → 1921 73.27 (1920→21 −16.0%, 1919→21 −5.1%). Real GNP 507.1 → 496.3 → 478.8 (1920→21 −3.5%, 1919→21 −5.6%) (calculated value).
- Balke–Gordon Table 5 (JPE p.64, 1919=100): real GNP for 1920/1921/1922/1923 is Commerce 92.2/85.7/98.3/109.1, Kendrick 98.9/96.6/102.2/115.8, Kuznets variant A 100.7/94.9/101.5/115.7, Kuznets variant B 96.0/87.9/94.7/107.3. Hours worked (Kendrick) 99.3/89.8/96.1/103.5. Real GNP per hour is 101.4/105.7/105.6/111.8 for variant A and 96.7/97.9/98.5/103.6 for variant B.
- From these, the 1919→21 output decline diverges: Kendrick −3.4%, Kuznets variant A −5.1%, Kuznets variant B −12.1%, Commerce −14.3% (calculated value).
- Ratio of price decline to output decline: Commerce 2.6 (18/6.9), Balke–Gordon 3.7 (13/3.5), Romer 6.3 (14.8/2.35) (calculated value). The larger the ratio, the smaller the output decline relative to the deflation.

**B–G's doubts** (JPE pp.64–65): in Kuznets variant A (the foundation of Kendrick), output per hour rises about 4% in 1920→21 (101.4 → 105.7). Productivity usually falls in a short downturn, yet over the same two years manufacturing hours fell 28.3% and transportation 12.2% (citing Kendrick 1961). B–G point out that the implication is problematic: the fall in manufacturing hours amounts to "nearly two-thirds" of the 1929–33 great contraction, yet the rest of the economy is implied to have been unaffected, and they write that they do not know which series is correct. By the source note to the table, the 1919–21 values in Table 10 are the standard GNP (Kendrick) trend plus the mean deviations of rows 3c and 4c of Table 9, so they are based on the Kendrick series.

**Money stock** (Anderson 2003, Table 3, F–S series annual averages): M1 23.73 in 1920 → 21.51 in 1921 (−9.4%), M2 34.80 → 32.85 (−5.6%), M4 40.86 → 40.61 (−0.6%). For 1919 → 1921, M1 is 21.79 → 21.51 (−1.3%) (calculated value). Compared with the −14.8% fall in prices over the same period (Romer deflator), real M2 is about +11% (0.944/0.852). Because this compares different measures, only the direction should be taken from it (inference).

**Policy and aftermath.** F–S 1982 (pp.154–155) mention the Fed's tightening in 1920. The BIS (2015, footnote 6) writes that the US had a weak price rise in 1923–26 after the sharp deflation of the early 1920s. Also, under the BIS peak rule, the 1920 peak is excluded from the five-year comparison in Graph 3 because its preceding five years overlap with World War I years (2-2.5).

**Interpretation**: the statement "the output loss in 1920–21 was small" rests on the Romer and Kendrick series. With Commerce or Kuznets B it is much larger. So the safe wording is "short and violent," not "harmless."

### 2-4.3 The US and the world, 1929–33: bad deflation

**1. Output, prices, employment** (Bernanke 2004, *Money, Gold, and the Great Depression*; BIS 2015, Box 1):

- Real output: Bernanke about −30% (1929–33). The BIS writes that US real output fell by about 1/3. The roughly 3 pp difference between the two figures appears to come from differences in data source and aggregation (inference).
- Consumer prices: the BIS says they fell about 1/4 in the US. Bernanke says prices fell nearly 10% a year in the early 1930s, which is the rate in the peak years.
- Nominal wages: the BIS says manufacturing hourly wages fell about 1/5, and the unemployment rate went from 3% to 25%.
- Figures compiled by Friedman·Schwartz (quoted from a university lecture page, secondary source): implicit prices down by more than 1/4, wholesale down by more than 1/3, constant-price NNP down by more than 1/3.
- 15-country comparison (BIS, Box 1): median output about −7% (1929–33), median prices about −18%, housing prices about −22%, equities −51% (from the 1928 peak to 1931; US −67%). By 1938, median per-capita output was about 12% higher than in 1929, but in the US it was about 11% lower. The BIS's summary is that what was distinctive about this crisis was not the size of the price decline but the coincidence of the US output collapse, banking collapse, and asset price crash (Box 1, conclusion p.48).

**2. Banks**: Bernanke (2004) writes that about half of US banks were closed or merged between December 1930 and March 1933. A university lecture page, by contrast, says the number of operating commercial banks fell by more than 1/3, which appears to reflect different counting bases, net operating count versus closures and mergers (inference). Bernanke's account is that the decline in the money stock came not from a shrinking monetary base but from bank failures and the hoarding of cash.

**3. Money stock: monthly basis** (Friedman·Schwartz 1963, *A Monetary History*, Appendix Table A-1, printed pp.712–713, millions of dollars, seasonally adjusted):

- Table columns: col.1 currency, col.2 adjusted commercial bank demand deposits, col.4 total commercial bank deposits, col.7 = col.1 + 2 (M1), col.8 = col.1 + 4 (F–S's M2-type), col.9 = col.1 + 4 + 5 + 6 (M3-type).
- M1: 28,264 in October 1929 → 19,039 in April 1933, −32.6%. But October 1929 is a one-month spike (26,415 in September, 25,503 in November). From the September base, 19,039/26,415 gives −27.9% (calculated value). Demand deposits (col.2) are 22,593 in September → 24,432 in October → 21,651 in November.
- Currency + total commercial bank deposits (col.8): 46,278 in August 1929 → 29,970 in March 1933, −35.2%. To the lowest month, April 1933 at 29,747, it is −35.7%. From the spike value of 48,155 in October 1929 to April 1933 it is −38.2% (calculated value).
- Total commercial bank deposits (col.4): 42,359 in August 1929 → 24,461 in March 1933, −42.3%. Currency (col.1): 3,919 → 5,509, +40.6%.
- Just before the 1933 bank holiday: total deposits fell from 27,019 in February 1933 to 24,461 in March (−9.5%), and from 29,175 in January 1933 to 24,461 in March (−16.2%), a decline concentrated in two months (calculated value).

**4. Money stock: annual average basis** (Anderson 2003, Table 3, F–S series, billions of dollars, 1929 → 1933):

- Currency 3.90 → 5.09 (+30.5%), demand deposits 22.74 → 14.82 (−34.8%), M1 26.64 → 19.91 (−25.3%), total commercial bank deposits 42.70 → 27.13 (−36.5%), M2 46.60 → 32.22 (−30.9%), M3 55.20 → 41.52 (−24.8%), M4 60.99 → 48.09 (−21.1%). The peaks are all in 1929 and the troughs all in 1933.
- Currency's share of M2 rose from 8.4% in 1929 (3.90/46.60) to 15.8% in 1933 (5.09/32.22) (calculated value). Deposits turned into cash.
- Definitions (Anderson's text): F–S M1 is currency held by the non-bank public plus commercial bank demand deposits. F–S M2 is currency plus total commercial bank deposits. M3 is M2 plus mutual savings bank and postal savings deposits. M4 is M3 plus savings and loan shares. F–S M2 resembles no currently published measure, and F–S M4 is similar to the current Fed M2. The Fed's official M1, M2, and M3 begin in 1959. The annual averages are constructed from F–S's mostly end-of-period data.
- **Summary**: "the money stock fell by about one-third" is close to M1 −33% and M2-type −35% on a monthly peak-to-trough basis, and to M2 −31% and total deposits −36% on an annual average basis. M1 fell less because currency rose, and M4 (−21%), which includes savings banks and savings and loan associations, fell least. This document's interpretation is that the monetary collapse of the Great Depression was a collapse of commercial bank deposits, and that people converted deposits into cash. This dependence on definition is the starting point of the interlude "What is money? (M1·M2)."

**5. Same event, different explanations**

- Monetary and banking explanation: the Friedman·Schwartz line and Bernanke (2004).
- Credit explanation: Bernanke (1983), Calomiris·Mason (2003a).
- Counterargument: Calomiris·Mason (2003b) see most bank failures as explained by fundamentals, with the upper bound of the panic effect at about 600 days in Figure 4.
- Productivity: Cole·Ohanian·Leung (2005) see about 2/3 of the output change as a productivity shock.
- Gold standard: Ellison et al. (2020), Bernanke (2004), Eichengreen·Sachs (1985).
- Bernanke's (2004) account is that recovery began after Roosevelt left the gold standard and ended the banking crisis (bank holiday, deposit insurance), and Card C-G covers the delayed recovery.

### 2-4.4 Japan 1998–2012: weak but long

**Prices.** BIS (2015, Box 2): the cumulative fall in consumer prices in 1998–2012 was only 4%, but it lasted more than ten years. This corresponds to about −0.3% a year (calculated value). Nakaso (2014) says CPI averaged −0.3% a year in FY1998–FY2012 and persisted for 15 years. Academic journal overviews and a University of Tokyo CARF paper describing the same account give "around 1% a year," which appears to be the typical or maximum annual decline, but we could not confirm the index (inference). Chart 1 of Nakaso's chart collection reportedly shows "CPI (all items less fresh food)" adjusted for the consumption tax. The link between the −0.3% in the speech text and this series is an inference.

**Course of events.** The BIS (2015, Box 2) sees the asset and credit boom of the late 1980s ending in the stock and land price crash of the early 1990s, which damaged bank balance sheets and began the "lost decade," with deflation coming after that. Nakaso (2014): policy rate 6% (August 1990) → 0.25% (September 1998), zero interest rate policy (February 1999), quantitative easing (March 2001), QQE (April 2013). There is also an account that financial intermediation was impaired by the 1997 financial crisis.

**Growth.** BIS (2015, Box 2): real per-capita GDP grew a cumulative 6% in 1991–2000 (US 26%) and a cumulative 10% in 2000–13 (US about 12%). Per working-age person, it was more than 20% in 2000–13 (US about 11%), and in 2000–07 it was 9% per capita (US 11%) and 15% per working-age person (US 8%). The BIS's account is that the picture changes when one controls for population aging as a constraint on growth in the 2000s. A–K also see Japan's growth slowdown in the 1990s as the fading of catch-up growth (1.41; US 3.20, Italy 1.61, France 1.84).

**Expectations.** Nakaso (2014): as deflation lengthened, expectations hardened (deflationary equilibrium) and the practice of raising base pay disappeared for a time. Even so, he judges that the vicious circle was avoided because real GDP grew at an average annual rate of 1.5% in 2000–07. The CARF paper's premise is reportedly that Japanese firms came to take leaving prices unchanged as the default. Bernanke (2002), citing Fed research, summarizes that Japan's deflation was almost entirely unanticipated.

**Profile.** Weak decline, long duration, banking problems after an asset collapse, zero lower bound. The opposite of 1929–33. It shows that "harmful" can mean not collapse but long stagnation (interpretation). The interest of Nakaso's speech, as that of a central bank official defending QQE, must be taken into account.

### 2-4.5 China 2023–26: an observation note, not a case

- PPI fell for 40 consecutive months through January 2026 (−1.4%), −0.9% in February, and −2.6% for 2025 as a whole (secondary sources such as Trading Economics). CPI was about −0.3% year on year in September 2025 (UOB, via FXStreet).
- NBS (released October 14, 2026, via Xinhua, gov.cn, CGTN, SMM): in September 2026 CPI was +0.9% (the January–September average was also +0.9%), core CPI +1.0%, and PPI +5.0%, the fastest rise since June 2022. Food at −1.7% (pork about −10%) held CPI down; consumer goods were −0.1% and producer goods +6.5%. The NBS statistician cited energy, coal, and industrial consumer goods prices as the reasons.
- Assessments are divided (CNBC, October 14, 2026, citing Reuters). Zhiwei Zhang of Pinpoint sees domestic demand as still weak, while Hao Zhou of Guotai Junan sees the economy gradually emerging from deflationary pressure.
- Interpretation: the producer-side rebound owes much to cost factors, and consumer-side demand is weak. This document does not judge which it is. China, which the BIS (2015) counted as one of the four postwar cases, was an episode running through 2013, and we have not verified whether the 2023–25 decline qualifies as BIS-style "persistent" (five-year cumulative decline). The figures above are flash estimates and may be revised later.

### 2-4.6 Korea 2019: a reference case

Korea is not among the four postwar persistent deflations in BIS 2015 (Korea's only CPI peak in Table A3 is 1920). What follows compiles Statistics Korea announcements and press reports.

- August 2019: official year-on-year 0.0%, raw figure −0.038% (Statistics Korea's rule of publishing to one decimal place).
- September 2019: 105.2 (2015=100), year-on-year −0.4%. The first official negative since consumer price compilation began in 1965 (year-on-year figures are compiled from 1966).
- January–September average 0.4% (citing a National Assembly Budget Office report), 2019 annual average 0.4%. The month-on-month change in September was +0.4%, so it was not a consecutive decline.
- Causes: the base effect from farm product prices that rose in the summer 2018 heat wave, petroleum products −5.6%, falls in administered prices from welfare policies, and so on. Statistics Korea, the Ministry of Economy and Finance, the Bank of Korea, and KDI summarized it as "not deflation."
- Afterward: October official 0.0% (raw figure slightly positive), November +0.2%, December +0.7%, ending as a transitory decline.
- Interpretation: in the BIS framework, it is a transitory, not persistent, decline. The authorities' "supply factor" explanation follows the same logic as the BIS's good/bad deflation distinction, but the weight of demand factors cannot be judged from press reports alone.

## 2-5. Why the figures diverge

The same framework as 10.0 in Part 1. A: the object itself is not a single value (reference date, window). B: the indexes, methods, and definitions differ. C: insufficient data and transcription errors.

- **1870s real growth 6.8% vs 4.3–4.9% vs 3.6% vs 4.5% vs 5.4% vs 5.6%**: the Kuznets series, F–S's money-based back-calculation, F–S's adopted 1867–79.2 value, Balke–Gordon, Romer, F–S 1982 stage data. Differences in how the series were constructed. B.
- **1870s prices −3.8% vs −3.5% vs −3.2% vs −5.4% vs −6½%**: implicit NNP prices, F–S's adopted figure, Balke–Gordon·Romer deflators, wholesale prices (monthly, 1867.1–79.2), wholesale prices (1865–79). Differences in index and period. B.
- **1920–21 output −2.4% to −14.3%**: Romer, B–G Table 10, Kendrick, Kuznets A·B, Commerce. Choice of series. B.
- **1920–21 prices −13% to −36.8%**: B–G, Romer, Commerce, wholesale prices. Differences in index. B.
- **1929–33 money stock −21% to −42%**: M1·M2·M3·M4·total deposits, monthly peak-to-trough versus annual average. A + B.
- **1929–33 prices −1/4 vs −1/3 vs −10% a year**: the BIS's CPI, wholesale prices, the rate in Bernanke's peak years. A + B.
- **1929–33 banks "half" vs "more than 1/3"**: closures and mergers versus net operating count. B.
- **BIS US peak 1926 (2004) vs 1920 (2015)**: the 2015 edition added "higher than the five years before and after," and footnote 6 does not treat the 1923–26 rise as a separate peak. B (rule difference).
- **Cole·Ohanian·Leung's "24% deflation"**: reading the log changes in Table 2 (−0.27, −0.24) as simple percentages. As simple percentages they are −23.7% and −21.3%. A.
- **Japan −0.3% a year vs cumulative −4% vs "around 1%"**: the annual average and the cumulative figure agree (−0.3%/yr ≈ −4%). The "1%" has an unconfirmed index and basis. A + C.
- **Korea August 2019 −0.04% vs 0.0%**: raw figure versus official release (rounded to one decimal place). A.
- **Atkeson·Kehoe "21" vs "8"**: a contradiction within the same paragraph, resolved by arithmetic. C.

One lesson carries over from Part 1. Deflation figures also depend on which index, which period, and which unit.

## 2-6. Summary and confidence by layer

**In one paragraph.** In these cases, a fall in prices itself is a weak predictor of depression (Atkeson·Kehoe: 65 of 73 deflations outside the Great Depression had no depression. BIS: growth is higher in deflation years in the postwar period). Where it was harmful, it was a combination rather than a single factor. The decline was large and unanticipated, nominal debt was heavy, asset prices and credit collapsed, banks and the money stock collapsed, and policy was constrained (gold standard, zero lower bound). Fisher (1933) himself wrote that the cases with only debt or only deflation are much weaker. Which combination is a necessary condition has not been settled. The Japanese type (weak, long stagnation) and the 1929–33 type (sharp fall) are different profiles, and there is no basis for thinking their causes are the same.

**Confidence by layer.**

- A fall in prices alone is a weak predictor: medium to high. The two studies' definitions of depression differ, and A–K do not control for whether deflation was anticipated.
- The strength of the Great Depression link is also disputed: low to medium. A–K slope 0.40 (0.28). Cole·Ohanian·Leung hold the opposite.
- Asset prices + private debt matter more than goods prices: medium. The sample is small and the debt analysis is exploratory. It is in tension with Fisher's goods price path.
- Monetary and banking collapse made 1929–33 unusually severe: medium to high. The cause of bank failures is disputed (Calomiris·Mason: fundamentals-centered).
- The gold standard mattered: medium to high. Given that 1873–96 was favorable, it is "regime × conditions."
- The delayed recovery of the 1930s was due to wage and cartel policy: low to medium. Model-dependent.
- Japan as a distinct weak-but-long type: medium. The population and catch-up effects need to be separated, and Nakaso is an interested party.
- "Good deflation" as a general category: low to medium. The authors themselves warn it is simple.

## 2-7. Open questions

- Why were the 1930s different? Candidates are a combination of demand collapse, nominal debt, collapsed banks, an asset price crash, and a rigid monetary regime.
- Money or credit, and were bank failures fundamentals or panic? Calomiris·Mason (2003b) find a large unexplained increase only in early 1933.
- Were wages rigid? Wages fell by about 1/5, and Mishkin sees less rigidity in deflation, but Cole·Ohanian see wages as too high after 1933.
- Is 1920–21 a counterexample or a special case? Output estimates diverge from −2.4% to −14.3%.
- Is Fisher's path goods prices or asset prices?
- Why was Japan weak yet persistent? Entrenched expectations, or population and catch-up effects?
- Is China's PPI rebound a recovery in demand or a cost factor? Is 2023–25 BIS-style "persistent"?
- Was the 1870s deflation supply-led (F–S's output expansion) or a monetary path of preparation for returning to the gold standard (F–S 1982)?
- The connection to ordinary times is for Part 3. Mishkin (2008) holds that with a target inflation of about 1% or more, the frequency of the zero lower bound falls sharply, and at about 2% it is almost unconstraining. By contrast, the Bank of Canada model holds that wage rigidity can reduce this need.
- This part's money stock result (diverging from −21% to −42% depending on definition) leads into the interlude "What is money? (M1·M2)."
