Navier–Stokes Applications Lab

Hands-on labs for blood flow, Rossby waves, cavitation, SST closure, and more. [Blood flow] Solves pulsatile flow in a rigid circular tube directly from the axisymmetric Navier–Stokes equation. Computes non-Newtonian (Carreau–Yasuda) viscosity, wall shear stress (WSS), TAWSS·OSI·RRT, and a comparison against the Womersley analytical solution for the Newtonian limit, all in real time.

Vessel cross-section (axial velocity; orange = forward, blue = reverse flow)

Radial velocity profile

Driving pressure gradient G(t) · mean velocity Ū(t)

Wall shear stress τ_w(t) (shaded = reverse direction)

Model. ρ∂u/∂t = G(t) + (1/r)∂/∂r[r μ(γ̇) ∂u/∂r], G(t)=G₀+G₁cos ωt, no-slip wall, symmetric about the axis. Radial finite volume (80 cells) + backward Euler, iterated until a periodic steady state is reached. μ(γ̇)=μ∞+(μ₀−μ∞)[1+(λγ̇)^a]^((n−1)/a), μ₀=0.16 Pa·s, μ∞=0.0035 Pa·s, λ=8.2 s, a=0.64, n=0.2128, ρ=1060 kg/m³. The analytical solution in Newtonian mode is the Womersley solution u=G₀(R²−r²)/(4μ)+Re[(G₁/iωρ)(1−J₀(αi^{3/2}r/R)/J₀(αi^{3/2}))e^{iωt}] .
Limitations. Assumes a rigid tube, fully developed, axisymmetric flow, so secondary flow at bifurcations, wall elasticity, and patient-specific geometry are not included. For educational use only; not for clinical decisions. Preset values are examples, not representative literature values. WSS threshold values (e.g., τ_w < 0.4 Pa) differ between studies.

Phase speed c_p · group velocity c_gx vs. wavenumber m

Dispersion relation ω = Uk − βk/(k²+ℓ²+F), β=2Ωcosφ/a, zonal length L_x=2πa·cosφ. Increasing U until c_p=0 gives a stationary Rossby wave (the basis of blocking theory). This is a linear single plane wave, so nonlinear interactions are not included.

Venturi geometry (red section: p<p_v) and Bernoulli pressure

Water at 20 °C (p_v=2338 Pa, ρ=998 kg/m³). Inception is judged by comparing the inviscid Bernoulli pressure with p_v; cavity growth and post-choking dynamics (Schnerr–Sauer mass transfer) were handled in a separate computation code.

Gas particles in the nozzle (color = Mach number: blue subsonic · white M=1 · red supersonic)

Axial M · p/p₀ · T/T₀ (vertical line = throat)

A quasi-1D isentropic flow of a perfect gas (R=345 J/kg·K, A_t=0.01 m²) plus a normal-shock model. Particle speed is proportional to the local velocity u=M·√(γRT) (the speed scale is for display only), and the shape of the jet outside the exit is qualitative. Vorticity field ω (red = counterclockwise, blue = clockwise) — Kármán vortex street behind a cylinder

A direct 2D lattice Boltzmann (D2Q9, BGK) computation. 320×128 grid, cylinder diameter D=22 lattice units (17% blockage), inlet U=0.1 (Ma≈0.17); the cylinder uses bounce-back, the top, bottom, and inlet use free-stream equilibrium distributions, and the outlet uses extrapolation. Because of the blockage (D/H=17%), the measured St comes out higher than free-stream empirical formulas, so it is compared using the approximate correction St·(1−D/H). Real flow develops 3D instabilities at Re≳190, so above that this 2D result is for reference only.

Bubble (scaled by radius R(t)/R₀)

R(t)/R₀ · wall velocity Ṙ (normalized)

Rayleigh–Plesset equation RṘ̈+(3/2)Ṙ²=(1/ρ)[p_v+p_g0(R₀/R)^{3κ}−p_∞−4μṘ/R−2σ/R], p_∞=101.3 kPa, κ=1.4(adiabatic), water at 20 °C. Because the liquid is assumed incompressible, results are inaccurate near the minimum radius where the wall speed exceeds about 30% of the speed of sound (a compressible model such as Keller–Miksis is needed), and heat transfer, phase change, and bubble shape collapse (jetting) are not included. Treat the gas pressure estimate only as an upper bound of the model.

Temperature field T (red = hot, blue = cold) and fluid particles — Rayleigh–Bénard convection heated from below

2D Boussinesq equations (vorticity–streamfunction form), free-slip fixed-temperature top and bottom boundaries, periodic in x, aspect ratio 4√2:1 (twice the critical wavelength 2√2). Second-order central differences + Heun; the Poisson equation is solved directly with FFT + tridiagonal solves. Critical value for free-slip boundaries Ra_c=27π⁴/4≈657.5. Because central differencing is used, the grid is too coarse above Ra≳2×10⁴ and results are inaccurate. The approximate theoretical Nu, 1+2(1−Ra_c/Ra), is valid only near the critical point.

Flow around an airfoil section (Joukowski potential flow + Kutta condition)

Pressure coefficient −Cp (suction on the upper surface points up)

Inviscid, incompressible 2D potential flow. The section is built from the flow around a circle using the Joukowski map Z=ζ+1/ζ, and the circulation Γ=4πR·sin(α−φ) is fixed by the Kutta condition that the velocity be finite at the trailing edge. Because there is no viscosity, there is no boundary layer, separation, stall, or drag (a real wing stalls at roughly 12–16°). The particle line segments indicate the local velocity direction.

Vorticity field ω (red = counterclockwise, blue = clockwise) — 2D incompressible flow

Solves the 2D vorticity equation ∂ω/∂t+u·∇ω=ν∇²ω on a 64×64 periodic domain with a pseudo-spectral method (2/3 dealiasing, RK4). In ideal flow (ν=0), the energy E=½⟨|u|²⟩ and enstrophy Z=½⟨ω²⟩ should be conserved, which you can check with E/E₀ and Z/Z₀ on screen (there is slight decay due to dealiasing and time integration).

Water surface h(x,t) — numerical solution (blue) vs. Stoker analytical solution (dashed)

Flow velocity u(x,t) — numerical vs. analytical

Solves the 1D shallow-water equations h_t+(hu)_x=0, (hu)_t+(hu²+gh²/2)_x=0 with 400-cell MUSCL (minmod) + Rusanov flux + SSP-RK2. The analytical solution is Stoker's wet-bed dam break (rarefaction wave + shock/bore). Friction, bed slope, 2D effects, and non-hydrostatic dispersion are not included.

Lorenz attractor (orange and blue: two trajectories that initially differ only by δ; slowly rotating)

Distance between the two trajectories ln|A−B| (exponential divergence)

The Lorenz (1963) model, which reduces thermal convection to three modes: ẋ=σ(y−x), ẏ=x(r−z)−y, ż=xy−βz (σ=10, β=8/3), RK4(dt=0.005). The Lyapunov exponent is computed with Benettin's method (renormalization). Convection begins at r=1, and beyond the Hopf bifurcation r_H=σ(σ+β+3)/(σ−β−1)≈24.74 the stable fixed points disappear and chaos appears (to my knowledge, transient chaos also exists at r below 24.74).

Water surface η(x,t)=cos(k₁x−ω₁t)+cos(k₂x−ω₂t) — phase speed vs. group velocity

Dispersion relation ω(k) (the two current wavelengths are marked)

Dispersion relation of linear gravity waves ω²=gk·tanh(kh). Phase speed c_p=ω/k, group velocity c_g=dω/dk (c_g=c_p/2 in deep water; c_g=c_p=√(gh) in shallow water). You can see the envelope (beats) formed by the sum of the two waves moving at the group velocity. Because this is linear theory, it does not hold when the wave height is large (nonlinearity, wave breaking).

Head H(x) along the pipeline (left = reservoir, right = valve)

Head at the valve H(t) (last 12 s)

A pipeline with L=1000 m, D=0.5 m is solved with the method of characteristics (MOC). The upstream end is a constant-head reservoir (H₀=100 m), and the downstream valve closes linearly. For rapid closure (t_c<2L/a), the result is compared with the Joukowsky head rise ΔH=aV₀/g; for slow closure, with the Michaud approximation ΔH≈2LV₀/(g·t_c). The period is 4L/a. Column separation (vapor cavities) and changes in pipe elasticity are not included, so any interval where the head drops below vapor pressure is for qualitative reference only.

Lid-driven cavity flow (color = speed; line segments = particles along streamlines)

Vertical centerline velocity u(y)/U — numerical (line) vs. Ghia et al. (1982) tabulated values (dots)

2D lattice Boltzmann (D2Q9, BGK), 128×128. The top lid moves at U=0.1 (Ma≈0.17) and the remaining walls use bounce-back (with moving-wall correction). It takes time to reach steady state (longer for larger Re), and the dots on the right are the tabulated values from Ghia, Ghia & Shin (1982) (Re=100, 400, 1000). Near Re=1000, BGK can show large errors if the grid is not fine enough.

Density field (blue = heavy fluid on top, orange = light fluid below) — Rayleigh–Taylor instability

Uses the same 2D Boussinesq solver as the thermal convection tab (vorticity–streamfunction, FFT Poisson), but with the background temperature gradient term removed and two layers placed — heavy on top, light below (tanh interface of thickness 0.08). The upper bound from linear theory (sharp interface, inviscid) is σ=√(A·g·k)=√(B·k/2); viscosity, diffusion, and the finite interface thickness reduce the growth rate. The larger B is, the closer it approaches this bound (in my calculation, about 0.85–0.88 times at B=4×10⁵). Because central differencing is used, results for B≳4×10⁵ or for thin filaments at late times are inaccurate due to grid limits.

Plane Poiseuille flow with a superimposed Tollmien–Schlichting wave (particles)

Linear stability map: Re–α (red = unstable, blue = stable)

Eigenfunctions |φ(y)|, |φ′(y)| (y=+1 → −1)

The Orr–Sommerfeld equation (D²−α²)²φ = iαRe[(U−c)(D²−α²)φ − U″φ], U=1−y² is solved with Chebyshev collocation (N=48; N=36 for the map) and Rayleigh-quotient inverse iteration. For the eigenvalue c=c_r+ic_i, the linear growth rate is αc_i. The known critical point is Re_c=5772.22, α_c=1.02056, c_c=0.26400 (my calculation: 5772.17). In linear theory, Re<5772 is stable at all wavenumbers, but real pipe flow can become turbulent at much lower Re because of finite-amplitude disturbances and 3D nonlinear transition; the particle view shows a fixed-amplitude wave with the growth factor removed.
